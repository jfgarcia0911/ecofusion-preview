## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

1. Compare and contrast major curriculum design frameworks

4. Align curriculum with industry standards and employer needs
5. Design coherent learning progressions from novice to expert

## Key Concepts

### 1. Curriculum Design Frameworks Overview

#### Traditional Subject-Based Design
**Structure:** Organized by academic disciplines
**Example:** Chemistry → Botany → Engineering → Business

**Strengths:**
- Clear academic organization
- Easy to staff with specialists
- Aligns with traditional education

**Weaknesses:**
- Fragmented knowledge
- Unclear application
- Doesn't mirror real-world problems

**Use in CEA:** Best for foundational academic programs

#### Integrated/Thematic Design
**Structure:** Organized around themes or topics
**Example:** "Optimizing Plant Growth" integrates chemistry, botany, engineering

**Strengths:**
- Shows connections between disciplines
- More relevant to practice
- Natural integration of knowledge

**Weaknesses:**
- Requires highly skilled instructors
- Can miss important foundational content
- Assessment can be complex

**Use in CEA:** Excellent for professional development and advanced courses

#### Competency-Based Design
**Structure:** Organized around skills and abilities to be demonstrated
**Example:** "Design and implement nutrient management systems"

**Strengths:**
- Clear, measurable outcomes
- Directly relevant to workplace
- Flexible learning pathways
- Credit for prior learning

**Weaknesses:**
- Time-intensive to develop
- Requires robust assessment systems
- May underemphasize theoretical understanding

**Use in CEA:** Ideal for certification programs and workforce training

#### Problem-Based Design
**Structure:** Organized around authentic problems to solve
**Example:** "How do we eliminate food deserts in urban areas?"

**Strengths:**
- High motivation and engagement
- Authentic application
- Develops critical thinking
- Real-world relevance

**Weaknesses:**
- Can miss systematic content coverage
- Requires significant facilitation skill
- Resource-intensive

**Use in CEA:** Powerful for capstone experiences and executive education

### 2. Backward Design (Understanding by Design)

**Philosophy:** Begin with the end in mind. Start with desired outcomes, then design assessment, then instruction.

#### Stage 1: Identify Desired Results

**Enduring Understanding:**
What do you want students to understand years after the course?

**Example - CEA Course:**
- *Enduring Understanding:* "Plant growth is the result of managing interdependent environmental variables; optimizing one factor without considering others leads to suboptimal results."
- This principle will guide decisions throughout their CEA career

**Essential Questions:**
Open-ended questions that frame the curriculum

**Example Questions for CEA:**
- How do we balance productivity with sustainability?
- What factors truly drive plant health and yield?
- How do we make data-driven decisions in complex systems?
- What does "optimization" mean in CEA contexts?

**Learning Objectives:**
Specific, measurable outcomes (covered in detail in Lesson 3)

**Example:**
Students will be able to:
- Diagnose nutrient deficiencies from visual symptoms
- Calculate optimal lighting schedules for specific crops
- Design climate control strategies for different seasons

#### Stage 2: Determine Acceptable Evidence

**Before designing instruction, determine how you'll know students have learned.**

**Assessment Types:**

1. **Performance Tasks:**
   - Design a complete growing system
   - Troubleshoot a failing crop
   - Conduct a facility energy audit

2. **Other Evidence:**
   - Quizzes on foundational knowledge
   - Observation of laboratory skills
   - Written reflections on learning
   - Portfolio of work

**Key Principle:** Design assessment BEFORE instruction to ensure alignment.

#### Stage 3: Plan Learning Experiences

**Only after defining outcomes and assessments, design instruction.**

**WHERETO Framework:**

**W - Where and Why:**
- Where is the learning headed?
- Why is it important?
- What's the big picture?

**H - Hook and Hold:**
- How will you engage learners?
- What hooks their interest?
- How maintain engagement?

**E - Explore and Equip:**
- What experiences help learners explore?
- What instruction equips them?
- How do they make meaning?

**R - Reflect and Rethink:**
- What opportunities for reflection?
- How do they revise thinking?
- What feedback loops exist?

**E - Evaluate and Self-Assess:**
- How will they self-evaluate?
- What criteria for success?
- How do they track progress?

**T - Tailored:**
- How is instruction differentiated?
- How meet diverse needs?
- What choices are available?

**O - Organized:**
- How is learning sequenced?
- What's the optimal flow?
- How do pieces connect?

**Example Application - LED Lighting Module:**

**Stage 1 - Desired Results:**
- *Understanding:* Light quality affects plant development beyond just intensity
- *Essential Question:* How do we design lighting that optimizes both plant growth and energy efficiency?
- *Objectives:* Calculate PPFD requirements, select appropriate spectra, design layouts

**Stage 2 - Evidence:**
- *Performance Task:* Design complete lighting system for a new facility
- *Other Evidence:* Quiz on light physics, calculations worksheet, spectrum analysis

**Stage 3 - Learning Experience:**
- *W:* Tour facility with poor lighting; discuss production issues
- *H:* Experiment showing plants under different spectra
- *E:* Lecture on photobiology; hands-on light measurements
- *R:* Analyze own facility's lighting; peer feedback on designs
- *E:* Rubric for lighting designs; self-assessment checklist
- *T:* Choose crop of interest; optional advanced readings
- *O:* Progress from light basics → plant responses → system design

### 3. Competency-Based Education (CBE)

**Definition:** Education organized around competencies (knowledge, skills, abilities) with flexible time to achieve mastery.

#### Key Principles of CBE

1. **Competencies are transparent and measurable**
2. **Assessment is meaningful and authentic**
3. **Learners advance upon demonstrating mastery**
4. **Instruction is differentiated to meet needs**
5. **Learning outcomes are rigorous and relevant**
6. **Flexible pacing allows personalization**

#### Developing Competency Frameworks

**Step 1: Conduct Job/Task Analysis**
- What does a competent CEA professional do?
- What decisions do they make?
- What skills do they use daily?
- What knowledge is essential?

**Methods:**
- Interviews with employers
- Observation of workers
- Review of job descriptions
- Industry standard documents
- DACUM (Developing a Curriculum) workshops

**Step 2: Define Competency Statements**

**Format:** Action verb + object + context + standard

**Examples - CEA Competencies:**

1. **Technical Competencies:**
   - Design hydroponic systems that support commercial production with 95%+ plant survival
   - Diagnose and correct nutrient imbalances within 48 hours of symptom appearance
   - Operate and maintain climate control systems to maintain ±2°F and ±5% RH targets
   - Implement integrated pest management protocols reducing pesticide use by 75%

2. **Professional Competencies:**
   - Communicate technical information to non-technical stakeholders clearly and accurately
   - Analyze production data to make evidence-based management decisions
   - Train and supervise staff in standard operating procedures
   - Comply with food safety and environmental regulations

3. **Business Competencies:**
   - Calculate production costs and profitability for different crops
   - Develop and manage operational budgets
   - Create and execute marketing plans for products

**Step 3: Organize into Competency Domains**

**Example CEA Competency Framework:**

**Domain 1: Plant Production Systems (40%)**
- Hydroponics and soilless systems
- Crop selection and planning
- Propagation and transplanting
- Harvest and post-harvest

**Domain 2: Environmental Control (25%)**
- Climate management
- Lighting systems
- Irrigation and nutrition
- Air quality and CO2

**Domain 3: Operations Management (20%)**
- Standard operating procedures
- Quality assurance
- Staff management
- Supply chain

**Domain 4: Business Management (15%)**
- Financial planning
- Marketing and sales
- Regulatory compliance
- Strategic planning

**Step 4: Define Proficiency Levels**

**Novice → Advanced Beginner → Competent → Proficient → Expert**

**Example Progression - Nutrient Management:**

- **Novice:** Follow recipes; measure and mix under supervision
- **Advanced Beginner:** Adjust formulations with guidance; recognize major deficiencies
- **Competent:** Independently manage nutrition programs; diagnose and correct problems
- **Proficient:** Optimize formulations for specific conditions; predict and prevent issues
- **Expert:** Develop new formulations; mentor others; troubleshoot complex situations

**Step 5: Develop Assessment Methods**

Each competency needs valid, reliable assessment:
- Performance tasks
- Skills demonstrations
- Written exams
- Portfolio evidence
- Work-based assessment

**Step 6: Create Learning Pathways**

Multiple routes to competency:
- Courses and training
- Work experience
- Mentorship
- Self-study
- Prior learning assessment

### 4. Industry-Aligned Curriculum Development

**Goal:** Ensure curriculum prepares students for actual industry needs.

#### Industry Advisory Committees

**Composition:**
- CEA facility operators (5-7)
- Technology suppliers (2-3)
- Industry association representatives (1-2)
- Recent graduates/current students (2)
- Faculty/instructors (3-4)

**Functions:**
- Review and approve curriculum
- Provide industry insights
- Offer internship/job opportunities
- Guest lectures and mentorship
- Equipment donations
- Stay current with industry changes

**Meeting Schedule:** 2-3 times per year

**Best Practices:**
- Clear terms of reference
- Structured agenda
- Action items and follow-up
- Respect for their time
- Demonstrate impact of their input

#### Labor Market Analysis

**Data Sources:**
- Bureau of Labor Statistics
- Industry association reports
- Job posting analysis (Burning Glass, LinkedIn)
- Employer surveys
- Salary data

**Analysis Questions:**
- What positions exist in CEA?
- What skills are most in-demand?
- What credentials are required/preferred?
- What salary ranges are typical?
- Where are geographic centers of employment?
- What is projected growth?

**Example Findings:**
- "Grower/Horticulturist" is most common position (35% of postings)
- "Experience with climate control systems" appears in 67% of postings
- Bachelor's degree preferred but not required for most positions
- Average salary: $45,000-$65,000 depending on experience
- Concentrated in urban areas and near universities

#### Skills Gap Analysis

**Process:**
1. Survey current competencies of workforce
2. Survey desired competencies from employers
3. Identify gaps
4. Prioritize gaps by importance and size
5. Design curriculum to address gaps

**Example Gap:**
- *Current:* Workers understand plant nutrition theory
- *Desired:* Workers can troubleshoot automated fertigation systems
- *Gap:* Hands-on troubleshooting and equipment maintenance skills
- *Curriculum Response:* Add laboratory component with common equipment; troubleshooting scenarios

#### Credential Alignment

**Industry Certifications:**
- Link curriculum to existing certifications
- Prepare students for certification exams
- Potentially offer certification as part of program

**Examples:**
- Certified Crop Advisor (CCA)
- Certified Professional Horticulturist (CPH)
- OSHA Safety Certifications
- Food Safety Manager Certification
- Emerging CEA-specific certifications

**Academic Credentials:**
- Associate degrees (2 years)
- Bachelor's degrees (4 years)
- Graduate certificates (post-bachelor)
- Master's degrees

**Stackable Credentials:**
Design progression: Certificate → Associate → Bachelor → Master

### 5. Learning Progressions and Sequencing

**Learning Progression:** The pathway students follow from novice to expert understanding.

#### Principles of Sequencing

**1. Simple to Complex**
Start with basic concepts before advanced applications.

**Example:**
- Week 1: What is hydroponics?
- Week 5: Design a recirculating NFT system
- Week 10: Optimize system for specific economic and environmental constraints

**2. Concrete to Abstract**
Begin with tangible, observable phenomena before theoretical concepts.

**Example:**
- Start: Observe plants growing in different systems
- Middle: Measure and compare growth rates
- End: Understand theoretical principles of nutrient uptake

**3. Prerequisites**
Ensure foundational knowledge before building upon it.

**Example Prerequisite Chain:**
1. Plant anatomy → 2. Photosynthesis → 3. Light requirements → 4. Lighting system design

**4. Spiral Curriculum**
Return to concepts multiple times with increasing sophistication.

**Example - Climate Control:**
- Introduction Course: Basic thermostat operation
- Intermediate Course: Understanding VPD and integrating temperature/humidity
- Advanced Course: Modeling and optimizing full environmental systems

**5. Coherence and Integration**
Show connections between topics; avoid fragmentation.

**Example:**
Connect pest management (Module 5) back to climate control (Module 2) and plant stress (Module 3) to show relationships.

#### Scope and Sequence Documents

**Scope:** What content is covered (breadth and depth)
**Sequence:** In what order it's covered

**Example Scope & Sequence - CEA Fundamentals Course:**

| Week | Topic | Key Concepts | Prerequisites | Assessment |
|------|-------|--------------|---------------|------------|
| 1 | Introduction to CEA | CEA types, benefits, challenges | None | Quiz |
| 2 | Plant Biology Basics | Photosynthesis, respiration, transpiration | Week 1 | Quiz |
| 3 | Hydroponic Systems | DWC, NFT, ebb/flow | Week 2 | System comparison chart |
| 4 | Plant Nutrition | Macro/micro nutrients, EC, pH | Week 2, 3 | Mixing exercise |
| 5 | Lighting | PPFD, spectrum, photoperiod | Week 2 | Light measurement lab |
| 6 | Climate Control | Temperature, humidity, VPD, CO2 | Week 2 | Climate plan project |
| 7 | Crop Selection | Crop requirements, economics | All previous | Crop plan |
| 8 | Midterm Exam | Weeks 1-7 | All previous | Exam + practical |

**Visual representation showing:**
- Content coverage across courses

## Curriculum Design Process

### Step-by-Step Framework

**Phase 1: Analysis (Weeks 1-4)**
1. Conduct needs assessment
2. Analyze learner characteristics
3. Review industry requirements
4. Identify resources and constraints
5. Form advisory committee

**Phase 2: Design (Weeks 5-12)**
1. Define program goals and competencies
2. Create learning outcomes
3. Design assessments
4. Develop curriculum map
5. Sequence content
6. Identify instructional strategies
7. Plan resources and materials

**Phase 3: Development (Weeks 13-24)**
1. Write detailed syllabi
2. Create lesson plans
3. Develop instructional materials
4. Create assessments and rubrics
5. Produce or acquire resources
6. Develop student materials

**Phase 4: Implementation (Ongoing)**
1. Pilot test with small group
2. Gather feedback
3. Revise based on feedback
4. Full implementation
5. Support instructors
6. Monitor student progress

**Phase 5: Evaluation (Continuous)**
1. Assess student learning
2. Gather stakeholder feedback
3. Review program data
4. Make improvements
5. Document outcomes
6. Report to stakeholders

## Case Study: Designing a CEA Certificate Program

### Context
A community college wants to develop a CEA certificate program to serve local vertical farming companies.

### Application of Framework

**1. Needs Assessment Findings:**
- 5 local CEA companies hiring
- Need entry-level growers/technicians
- High school graduates, career changers
- Must complete in 1 year part-time
- Prefer hands-on learning
- Need pest management and automation skills

**2. Design Approach:**
- Competency-based framework
- Backward design for courses
- Hands-on, problem-based instruction
- Part-time evening/weekend schedule
- Industry partnerships for labs

**3. Competency Framework:**
**Domain 1:** Plant Production (6 competencies)
**Domain 2:** Systems Operations (5 competencies)
**Domain 3:** Crop Management (4 competencies)
**Domain 4:** Professional Skills (3 competencies)

**4. Curriculum Structure:**
- 4 courses (8 weeks each, 32 weeks total)
- Each course = 1 domain
- Mix of online and in-person
- Capstone practicum experience

**5. Backward Design Example - Course 1:**

**Stage 1: Desired Results**
- Competency: "Independently operate hydroponic systems"
- Understanding: "Plant roots need oxygen, water, and nutrients; system design affects delivery"
- Essential Question: "How do we design systems that reliably deliver what roots need?"

**Stage 2: Evidence**
- Performance: Design and operate a system for 8 weeks; healthy plants = competent
- Other: Quiz on systems, lab observations, written troubleshooting scenarios

**Stage 3: Learning Experiences**
- Visit commercial facilities
- Build small-scale systems
- Experiment with variables
- Troubleshoot problems
- Design for specific crops

**6. Industry Alignment:**
- Advisory committee reviews curriculum
- Companies provide equipment
- Students intern at facilities
- Curriculum prepares for emerging certifications

**7. Assessment:**
- Portfolio of work
- Performance demonstrations
- Written exams
- Employer feedback on interns

**8. Continuous Improvement:**
- Annual advisory committee review
- Student outcome tracking (job placement)
- Graduate surveys at 6 months, 1 year
- Curriculum updates annually

## Common Curriculum Design Mistakes

1. **Starting with Content Instead of Outcomes**
   - Don't start with "I want to cover topics X, Y, Z"
   - Do start with "Students need to be able to do A, B, C"

2. **Coverage Mentality**
   - Don't try to cover everything
   - Do prioritize enduring understanding and essential skills

3. **Ignoring Assessment Until the End**
   - Don't design instruction then figure out assessment
   - Do design assessment early to ensure alignment

4. **Lack of Coherence**
   - Don't create disconnected topics
   - Do show relationships and build progressively

5. **Unrealistic Resource Requirements**
   - Don't design curriculum you can't actually implement
   - Do consider facilities, equipment, expertise realistically

6. **Inflexibility**
   - Don't create rigid, one-path-only curricula
   - Do build in choices and multiple pathways

7. **Insufficient Industry Input**
   - Don't design in isolation from industry
   - Do involve employers throughout process

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **Backward design (outcomes → assessment → instruction) ensures alignment and effectiveness**
2. **Competency-based frameworks organize learning around real-world capabilities**
3. **Industry alignment requires ongoing partnerships and labor market analysis**
4. **Learning progressions guide students from novice to expert systematically**
5. **Curriculum maps show the big picture and ensure coherence**
6. **Multiple design frameworks exist; choose based on context and goals**
7. **Effective curriculum design is systematic, iterative, and data-driven**
