# Lesson 7: Media Relations and Interviews

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Build relationships with journalists and media professionals
- Pitch stories and expertise effectively
- Prepare for different types of media interviews
- Deliver clear, quotable soundbites
- Handle difficult questions and controversial topics
- Navigate crisis communication situations
- Leverage media coverage for maximum impact

## Introduction

Media coverage provides third-party credibility that self-promotion cannot match. When a respected journalist quotes you as an expert or features your insights in an article, it positions you as an authority in ways that paid advertising or social media never can.

This lesson provides strategies for building productive media relationships and delivering effective interviews that establish and reinforce your thought leadership position.

## Understanding the Media Landscape

### Types of Media Relevant to CEA

**Trade Publications**
- Greenhouse Grower, Urban Ag News, Produce Grower
- Industry-specific focus
- Practitioner audience
- Regular coverage opportunities

**Agricultural Mainstream Media**
- Farm Progress, Growing Produce, American Vegetable Grower
- Broader agricultural audience
- Occasional CEA coverage
- Larger reach

**Business and Technology Media**
- Forbes, Fast Company, TechCrunch
- Innovation and business focus
- CEA as emerging sector
- High-visibility opportunities

**Regional/Local Media**
- Newspapers, TV, radio
- Local angle emphasis
- Community interest stories
- Accessible opportunities

**Podcasts and Online Media**
- Growing platforms
- In-depth conversations
- Niche audience targeting
- Relatively easy access

### Media Motivations and Needs

**What Journalists Want:**
- Newsworthy stories
- Expert sources and quotes
- Accurate, verifiable information
- Timely responses
- Accessible explanations of complex topics
- Diverse perspectives

**What Journalists Don't Want:**
- Sales pitches disguised as news
- Jargon-heavy, inaccessible content
- Unresponsive sources
- Overly promotional material
- Self-serving quotes
- Restricted information

**Understanding Their Constraints:**
- Tight deadlines
- Multiple stories simultaneously
- Need for balance and objectivity
- Space/time limitations
- Editorial oversight

## Building Media Relationships

### Research and Targeting

**Identify Relevant Journalists:**

1. **Read regularly** - Know who covers CEA and agriculture
2. **Track bylines** - Note reporters who write about related topics
3. **Use Twitter** - Follow journalists, engage with their work
4. **Review mastheads** - Identify editors and section leads
5. **Monitor coverage** - See who covers competitors or peers

**Create Media Database:**
- Name and title
- Publication/outlet
- Beat/coverage area
- Contact information
- Social media handles
- Recent articles/interests
- Notes on interactions

### Making Initial Contact

**Warm Introduction Methods:**

**Comment on Their Work**
- Engage thoughtfully on social media
- Share their articles with insight
- Send brief thank-you for good coverage
- No ask, just genuine engagement

**Provide Value First**
- Share relevant research or data
- Offer expert sources (not just yourself)
- Alert to newsworthy developments
- Help without expectation

**Industry Event Networking**
- Introduce yourself at conferences
- Attend media receptions
- Brief, professional interaction
- Follow up appropriately

**Cold Outreach (When Necessary)**

Subject: Resource for [Topic They Cover]

Hi [Name],

I've been following your coverage of [specific topic/recent article].

I'm [credential] with expertise in [relevant area]. I thought you might find [specific data/insight/story angle] interesting for your coverage of [topic].

[2-3 sentences of value - data, insight, or angle]

Would this be useful for a future story? I'm happy to share more details or connect you with other sources.

Best regards,
[Name]

**Keep it brief, focused, and value-driven.**

### Nurturing Media Relationships

**Ongoing Engagement:**

**Be Responsive**
- Reply to inquiries quickly (within hours)
- Meet deadlines reliably
- Be available when they need you

**Provide Quality**
- Accurate information
- Clear, quotable comments
- Additional resources when helpful
- Professional follow-through

**Respect Boundaries**
- Don't pitch constantly
- Understand their workflow
- Accept "no" gracefully
- Don't be pushy

**Stay Visible**
- Engage on social media
- Share their work
- Occasional check-ins
- Provide newsworthy updates

**Build Trust**
- Be honest and transparent
- Admit when you don't know
- Correct errors promptly
- Respect off-the-record agreements

## Pitching Stories and Expertise

### What Makes a Story Newsworthy

**News Values:**

**Timeliness** - Current, relevant now
**Impact** - Affects many people or important outcomes
**Novelty** - New, unusual, first-time
**Conflict** - Controversy or competition
**Prominence** - Involves well-known entities
**Proximity** - Local relevance
**Human Interest** - Emotional connection

Strong pitches combine multiple news values.

### Types of Pitches

**Reactive Pitching**
Respond to breaking news or trends with your expertise

Example: Industry bankruptcy announced
Your pitch: Expert perspective on what it means for sector

**Proactive Pitching**
Initiate story ideas based on your work or insights

Example: You've compiled data on energy efficiency
Your pitch: Special report on reducing operating costs

**Expertise Positioning**
Offer yourself as go-to expert for specific topics

Example: Introduce yourself as resource for LED lighting questions
Your value: Can provide technical explanations and real-world examples

**Research or Data Pitching**
Share original research or proprietary data

Example: Survey results on grower challenges
Your pitch: Exclusive first look at industry trends

### Crafting Effective Pitches

**Pitch Structure:**

**Subject Line** (Crucial - determines if opened)
- Specific and newsworthy
- Not clickbait
- Under 50 characters

Examples:
- "Data: 73% of vertical farms struggle with profitability"
- "New LED tech cuts energy costs 40%"
- "Interview available: Profitable urban farm operator"

**Opening Paragraph** (Hook them immediately)
- Lead with most newsworthy element
- One or two sentences
- Make it immediately clear why this matters

**Body** (Provide essential details)
- Key data or facts
- Why this is timely
- What's unique or new
- Who's affected
- Available resources (quotes, images, data)

**Close** (Make next steps easy)
- Your availability
- Additional resources you can provide
- Contact information
- Thank them for consideration

**Example Pitch:**

Subject: Data reveals #1 cause of vertical farm failures

Hi [Name],

Analysis of 47 vertical farm closures over the past three years reveals that 68% failed not due to technology issues, but inadequate capital planning for operational ramp-up.

This contradicts industry assumption that tech failures drive closures and has important implications for current investment trends.

I have:
- Detailed data analysis available for exclusive use
- Case studies from three facilities
- Commentary from finance and operations perspectives
- Charts/graphs ready to share

I can provide whatever you need and am available for interview this week.

Best regards,
[Name]
[Contact]

**Keep pitches under 200 words.**

### Pitch Timing and Follow-Up

**Timing Considerations:**
- Send early morning (before 9 AM) or early afternoon (1-2 PM)
- Tuesday-Thursday typically best
- Avoid late Friday or Monday morning
- Consider news cycle and deadlines
- Be aware of industry event timing

**Follow-Up Protocol:**
- Wait 3-4 business days
- Single follow-up email
- Brief reminder with added value if possible
- Accept silence as "no" after follow-up

**What Not to Do:**
- Multiple follow-ups
- Phone calls unless invited
- Pushy or demanding tone
- "Just checking if you saw this"
- Take rejection personally

## Interview Preparation

### Pre-Interview Preparation

**Understand the Context:**
- What's the story angle?
- Who else are they interviewing?
- What publication/audience?
- How will you be quoted (attribution)?
- When will it publish?
- Deadline for your input?

**Prepare Key Messages:**

Identify 3-5 main points you want to make, regardless of questions asked:

Example Key Messages:
1. "Profitability in CEA requires operational excellence, not just technology"
2. "Energy efficiency comes from systems thinking, not individual components"
3. "Successful farms balance automation with skilled human expertise"

Find ways to bridge to these messages from various questions.

**Develop Supporting Points:**
- Specific data and statistics
- Concrete examples
- Quotable phrases
- Analogies for complex concepts

**Anticipate Questions:**
- What will they likely ask?
- What difficult questions might arise?
- What would you ask in their position?
- Prepare and practice answers

**Gather Materials:**
- Relevant data and sources
- Photos or visuals if helpful
- Background documents
- Contact information for follow-up

### The Bridging Technique

**Bridge** from the question asked to your key messages:

**Question:** "What do you think about [specific technology]?"

**Bridge:** "That's an interesting technology, and it relates to the larger question of how we make farms profitable. What we've found is..."

**Bridging Phrases:**
- "What's really important here is..."
- "The key issue is actually..."
- "Let me put that in context..."
- "That relates to..."
- "The bigger picture is..."

Use bridges to stay on message while still being responsive.

### Types of Interviews

**Email Interview**
**Pros:** Time to craft thoughtful responses, written record
**Cons:** Less personal, may be heavily edited
**Strategy:** Write clear, quotable responses; provide more than requested

**Phone Interview**
**Pros:** Convenient, allows for dialogue
**Cons:** No visual cues, easy to ramble
**Strategy:** Stand while talking (energizes voice), have notes visible, eliminate distractions

**Video Interview**
**Pros:** Personal connection, shows expertise
**Cons:** Technical issues possible, requires preparation
**Strategy:** Professional background, good lighting, look at camera, dress professionally

**In-Person Interview**
**Pros:** Best rapport building, richest conversation
**Cons:** Time commitment, travel required
**Strategy:** Bring materials, dress professionally, use body language effectively

**Live Broadcast**
**Pros:** Immediate reach, high visibility
**Cons:** No do-overs, time pressure
**Strategy:** Practice extensively, stay calm, keep answers concise

## Effective Interview Techniques

### Delivering Quotable Soundbites

**Soundbite Characteristics:**
- 10-20 words
- Complete thought
- Vivid language
- Memorable phrasing
- Clear meaning

**Weak:** "Our operational efficiency improvements were significant and we saw good results from the changes we implemented."

**Strong:** "We cut labor costs 30% by redesigning workflow—same production, better results."

**Soundbite Formulas:**

**Number + Action + Result**
"Three changes reduced our energy costs 40%."

**Before/After**
"We went from losing money to profitable in six months."

**Analogy**
"Trying to automate bad processes is like putting a turbocharger on a broken engine."

**Contrarian**
"Everyone chases automation; we focused on training. That's why we're profitable."

### Voice and Delivery

**Pace**
- Speak slightly slower than normal conversation
- Pause between major points
- Don't rush—silence is okay

**Tone**
- Conversational, not lecture-like
- Confident without arrogance
- Appropriate energy
- Authentic passion

**Clarity**
- Enunciate clearly
- Avoid filler words (um, uh, like, you know)
- Complete sentences
- Define jargon

**Energy**
- Enthusiasm without over-doing it
- Vocal variety (avoid monotone)
- Match importance of topics
- Smile when appropriate (affects voice even on phone)

### Handling Difficult Questions

**Types of Difficult Questions:**

**The "Gotcha" Question**
Designed to create controversy or contradiction

**Strategy:** Stay calm, acknowledge validity if appropriate, redirect to facts

"That's a complex issue. What the data actually shows is..."

**The Hypothetical**
"What would you do if..."

**Strategy:** Avoid speculation, focus on what you know

"I can't speculate on hypotheticals, but based on our experience..."

**The Loaded Question**
Contains assumption you disagree with

**Strategy:** Reject premise politely, reframe

"I don't agree with that premise. What we actually see is..."

**The Multiple-Part Question**
Several questions bundled together

**Strategy:** Address the part most aligned with your message, or ask for clarification

"That's really three questions. Let me address the most important one..."

**The Controversial Topic**
Forces you to take potentially divisive stance

**Strategy:** Acknowledge nuance, focus on areas of agreement, present balanced view

"There are valid perspectives on both sides. What I can speak to from experience is..."

### What Not to Say

**Avoid:**

**"No comment"** - Sounds like you're hiding something
Instead: "I don't have information on that, but what I can share is..."

**"Off the record..."** - Risky and often doesn't work as intended
Instead: Only share what you're comfortable seeing published

**Speculation or Guessing** - Damages credibility
Instead: "I don't have data on that" or "That's outside my expertise"

**Negative Comments about Others** - Unprofessional
Instead: Focus on your own perspective and data

**Overly Technical Jargon** - Confusing and unusable
Instead: Explain simply, use analogies

**Absolute Statements** - Easy to disprove
Instead: "Generally," "Often," "In most cases"

## Managing the Interview

### Taking Control Professionally

**You Can:**
- Ask to see questions in advance (they may or may not agree)
- Request specific attribution (expert in X, CEO of Y)
- Provide additional context or resources
- Correct yourself if you misspeak
- Ask for clarification of questions
- Pause to collect thoughts

**You Cannot:**
- Demand editorial approval
- Insist on seeing article before publication
- Control how they use your quotes
- Require they use certain statements
- Determine article angle or focus

### Post-Interview Follow-Up

**Immediately After:**
- Send thank-you email
- Provide any promised information
- Clarify anything you may have misstated
- Offer to be resource for future stories

**When Article Publishes:**
- Read carefully
- Share on social media
- Thank journalist publicly
- Add to media coverage page
- Note any errors for future reference

**If There Are Errors:**
- Assess severity
- Contact journalist politely if significant
- Request correction for factual errors
- Accept minor issues gracefully
- Learn for next time

## Crisis Communication

### When Things Go Wrong

**Crisis Scenarios:**
- Business failure or setback
- Contamination or safety issue
- Public controversy
- Regulatory problems
- Negative media coverage

**Crisis Communication Principles:**

**1. Respond Quickly**
Silence creates vacuum filled by speculation

**2. Be Transparent**
Share what you know, admit what you don't

**3. Take Responsibility**
If at fault, acknowledge and explain what you're doing

**4. Focus on Facts**
Stick to verifiable information

**5. Show Empathy**
Acknowledge impact on stakeholders

**6. Describe Action**
What you're doing to address situation

### Crisis Communication Template

**Statement Structure:**

1. **Acknowledge** the situation
"We are aware of [issue] that occurred on [date]."

2. **Express concern** for affected parties
"Our primary concern is [safety/impact on customers/etc.]."

3. **State facts** known
"Here's what we know: [factual information]."

4. **Describe action** being taken
"We have immediately [action taken] and are [ongoing action]."

5. **Commit** to transparency
"We will provide updates as we learn more and are committed to full transparency."

6. **Provide contact** for information
"For more information, contact [person/department]."

**Example:**

"We are aware of the product recall issued yesterday. Customer safety is our highest priority. We have confirmed that lettuce harvested on July 15-16 may have been exposed to contamination. We have immediately halted distribution, notified all customers, and are working with food safety authorities to determine the source. We will provide daily updates and are committed to full transparency throughout this process. For information, contact our customer service line at [number]."

### What Not to Do in Crisis

- Don't speculate or guess
- Don't blame others
- Don't minimize the issue
- Don't say "no comment"
- Don't hide or be unavailable
- Don't delay response
- Don't provide incomplete information that must be walked back
- Don't show defensiveness or anger

## Leveraging Media Coverage

### Maximizing Impact

**Before Publication:**
- Alert your network
- Prepare to share
- Have social posts ready

**When Published:**
- Share on all platforms
- Thank journalist and publication
- Tag relevant parties
- Highlight key quotes
- Create graphics with quotes

**After Publication:**
- Add to website media page
- Include in speaker materials
- Reference in other content
- Use in marketing materials
- Send to stakeholders and clients

### Building on Coverage

**One Article Can Generate:**
- Social media content (multiple posts)
- Newsletter feature
- Blog post expansion
- Presentation content
- Additional media opportunities
- Speaking invitations
- Client inquiries

**Repurpose Strategically:**
- LinkedIn article expanding on topic
- Twitter thread of key points
- Presentation using coverage as example
- Case study if article featured your work

## Key Takeaways

1. **Relationships before pitches**—invest in genuine connections with journalists.

2. **Serve their needs first**—provide value to journalists, not just promotion for yourself.

3. **Preparation is crucial**—know your messages, anticipate questions, practice responses.

4. **Be quotable**—deliver clear, concise soundbites that journalists can easily use.

5. **Quick response matters**—journalists work on tight deadlines; responsiveness builds trust.

6. **Leverage coverage fully**—maximize impact through strategic sharing and repurposing.

7. **Build long-term presence**—consistent expert positioning leads to regular opportunities.
