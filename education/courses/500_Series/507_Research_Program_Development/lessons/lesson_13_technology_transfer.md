# Lesson 13: Technology Transfer and Commercialization

## Learning Objectives
1. Navigate technology transfer office relationships and processes
2. Evaluate pathways for technology commercialization
3. Understand licensing strategies and deal structures
4. Support startup formation from research
5. Facilitate proof-of-concept and technology de-risking

## Introduction

Technology transfer translates research discoveries into products, services, and practices that benefit society. Understanding commercialization pathways, working effectively with technology transfer offices, and supporting entrepreneurial activity enable research leaders to maximize real-world impact of their work.

---

## 1. Technology Transfer Office Relationships

### Role of Technology Transfer Office (TTO)

**Core Functions**:
- Evaluate inventions for commercialization potential
- Protect intellectual property (patents, copyrights)
- Market technologies to potential licensees
- Negotiate license agreements
- Monitor licensee compliance and collect royalties
- Support startup formation
- Manage conflicts of interest

**TTO Organizational Models**:
- **University Office**: Internal department; serves entire institution
- **Foundation Model**: Separate non-profit foundation
- **Shared Services**: Multiple institutions share TTO
- **Outsourced**: Third-party manages some or all functions

### Working Effectively with TTO

**Early Engagement**:
- Inform TTO of potential inventions early (before publication!)
- Discuss commercialization potential
- Seek guidance on IP strategy
- Connect TTO with potential industry partners

**Invention Disclosure Process**:
1. Researcher completes invention disclosure form
2. TTO evaluates commercial potential and patentability
3. Prior art search conducted
4. Decision: Pursue patent, publish, or decline
5. If pursuing patent: Work with patent attorney on application
6. Ongoing: Marketing to potential licensees

**Supporting TTO Marketing**:
- Provide clear explanations of technology value
- Identify potential licensees or applications
- Present at technology showcases
- Network at conferences to identify partners
- Respond promptly to licensee questions

**Revenue Sharing**:
- Typical split: 30-40% to inventors; 30-40% to department/lab; 20-40% to institution
- Varies by institution policy
- Distributed after costs recovered

### Inventor Responsibilities

**What TTOs Expect**:
- Timely disclosure of inventions
- Cooperation in patent prosecution
- Engagement with potential licensees
- Technical due diligence support
- Training licensee personnel (sometimes)
- No unauthorized disclosure before patent filed

**What Inventors Can Expect**:
- Professional evaluation of inventions
- Protection of IP
- Marketing to potential licensees
- Negotiation of fair license terms
- Regular updates on status
- Revenue sharing per policy

### Common Challenges and Solutions

**Challenge**: TTO declines to pursue patent
- **Solution**: Understand rationale; accept decision or seek alternative protection (trade secret, defensive publication)

**Challenge**: No licensees interested
- **Solution**: Refine value proposition; expand outreach; consider different applications; be patient (can take years)

**Challenge**: Licensee negotiations stall
- **Solution**: Understand sticking points; find creative solutions; consider other licensees

**Challenge**: Inventor conflicts with TTO decisions
- **Solution**: Discuss concerns; seek to understand constraints; escalate to TTO director if needed; ultimately TTO has authority

## 2. Technology Commercialization Pathways

### Pathway Selection Framework

**Technology Readiness Level (TRL)** (1-9 scale):
- **TRL 1-3**: Basic research; proof of concept
- **TRL 4-6**: Technology development; prototyping; testing
- **TRL 7-9**: Demonstration; production; deployment

**Market Readiness Level (MRL)** (1-9 scale):
- Assesses commercial viability
- Market need, value proposition, competition, business model

**Pathway Depends On**:
- TRL/MRL of technology
- Industry sector and adoption barriers
- Capital requirements for further development
- Entrepreneurial talent available
- Existing company interest

### Commercialization Pathways

**1. License to Existing Company**

**When Appropriate**:
- TRL 4-6; proven concept
- Company has complementary products/capabilities
- Industry interest demonstrated
- Low capital requirements
- Faster path to market

**Process**:
1. TTO markets technology
2. Interested companies identified
3. Due diligence (NDA; technical presentations)
4. Negotiate license terms
5. Sign agreement and execute
6. Company develops and commercializes
7. Royalties paid to university

**Timeline**: 6 months to 3 years from invention to license

**Success Factors**:
- Clear value proposition
- Strong IP position
- Company capability to develop and market
- Reasonable license terms
- Ongoing inventor engagement

**2. Startup Formation**

**When Appropriate**:
- No existing company interested or capable
- Entrepreneur willing to lead
- Capital available (angels, VCs)
- TRL 5-7; requires significant development
- Large market opportunity

**Process**:
1. Identify entrepreneurial founder(s)
2. Negotiate license with startup (exclusive typically)
3. Company formation
4. Seed funding (grants, angels, founders)
5. Product development and testing
6. Series A funding (venture capital)
7. Growth and scale
8. Exit (acquisition or IPO)

**Timeline**: 5-10+ years from invention to successful startup

**Success Factors**:
- Strong founding team
- Significant market opportunity ($100M+)
- Fundable business model
- Defensible IP
- Ability to execute and pivot

**3. Open Source/Non-Exclusive Licensing**

**When Appropriate**:
- Maximize adoption and impact
- Low commercial exclusivity value
- Industry standards or platforms
- Social or environmental mission priority
- Technology requires widespread adoption

**Process**:
1. University releases technology openly or licenses non-exclusively
2. Make available through publications, repositories, or licenses
3. Industry adopts freely
4. Broad impact achieved; little/no revenue

**Examples**:
- Agricultural practices or protocols
- Software tools for research
- Databases or datasets
- Educational materials

**4. Direct Application by University**

**When Appropriate**:
- University operations benefit
- Education/extension application
- Mission-aligned services
- No external market

**Examples**:
- Improved campus operations
- Extension programs delivering technology
- Training programs or curricula

## 3. Licensing Strategies and Deal Structures

### License Types and Terms

**Exclusive License**:
- One licensee; no others
- Field of use may be limited (e.g., exclusive for food crops; non-exclusive for ornamentals)
- Appropriate when significant development investment required
- Higher value to licensee; higher royalties

**Non-Exclusive License**:
- Multiple licensees possible
- Appropriate for mature technologies; low adoption barriers
- Lower royalties; broader impact

**Sole License**:
- One licensee but licensor retains rights
- Less common; hybrid approach

### Financial Terms

**Upfront Fees**:
- Payment upon signing: $10K-$500K+ (varies widely)
- Often lower for startups ($5K-$25K)
- Larger for established companies ($50K-$500K)
- Covers initial technology transfer; validates commitment

**Running Royalties**:
- Percentage of net sales: 2-10% typical in agriculture
- Paid quarterly or annually
- Auditable by licensor
- Most significant revenue source if product successful

**Milestone Payments**:
- Payments upon achieving development or commercial milestones
- Example: $25K upon FDA approval; $50K upon first commercial sale; $100K upon $5M sales
- Incentivizes progress; shares risk

**Minimum Annual Royalties**:
- Minimum payment each year regardless of sales
- Ensures licensee diligence
- Escalates over time (e.g., $10K Year 1; $25K Year 2; $50K Year 3+)
- If not met, license may revert to non-exclusive or terminate

**Equity (Startups)**:
- University takes stock in lieu of large cash payments
- Typical: 2-10% of company
- Provides upside if company successful
- Risk: Most startups fail

**Reimbursement of Patent Costs**:
- Licensee reimburses past patent costs
- Ongoing patent costs paid by licensee
- Typical for exclusive licenses

### Performance Terms

**Diligence Milestones**:
- Specific development milestones with deadlines
- Example: Prototype completion by Month 12; pilot trials by Year 2; product launch by Year 4
- Failure to meet may trigger termination or reversion to non-exclusive

**Commercialization Requirements**:
- First commercial sale by specified date
- Annual sales minimums
- Ensures technology reaches market

**Field of Use / Territory Restrictions**:
- License limited to specific applications or geographic regions
- Example: Exclusive for North America; non-exclusive elsewhere
- Allows multiple licenses in different fields/territories

**Sublicensing**:
- Whether licensee can sublicense to others
- If allowed, royalties on sublicense income
- Approval requirements

**Reporting Requirements**:
- Quarterly or annual sales reports
- Development progress reports
- Sublicense notifications

### Negotiation Considerations

**University Perspective**:
- Maximize societal impact and adoption
- Fair return on research investment
- Protect research and education missions
- Manage risk and liability
- Maintain reputation

**Company Perspective**:
- Competitive commercial terms
- Exclusivity to protect investment
- Reasonable royalty rates
- Flexibility to adapt as market evolves
- Minimal ongoing obligations

**Common Trade-Offs**:
- Higher royalty ↔ Lower upfront
- Broader exclusivity ↔ Higher payments
- Aggressive milestones ↔ Lower financial terms
- Equity ↔ Cash (for startups)

## 4. Supporting Startup Formation

### University Startup Ecosystem

**Entrepreneurship Support Services**:
- Entrepreneurship courses and programs
- Business plan competitions
- Incubator/accelerator programs
- Mentoring from entrepreneurs-in-residence
- Gap funding or proof-of-concept grants
- Legal and business formation support
- Networking with investors

**Faculty/Researcher Role**:
- Technical advisor to startup (not employee typically)
- Provide expertise during development
- May serve on scientific advisory board
- Validate technical milestones
- Recruit students/postdocs (with appropriate management)
- Champion technology to investors

**Conflict of Interest Management**:
- Disclose relationships to institution
- Management plan approved
- Time commitment limits (e.g., 1 day per week maximum)
- Students not required to work for startup
- Separate university and startup activities
- No university resources for startup (unless compensated)

### Startup Licensing Considerations

**Startup-Friendly Terms**:
- Lower upfront fees ($5K-$25K)
- Equity in lieu of large cash (2-10%)
- Reduced royalties during development (e.g., 2-3% vs. 5%+)
- Milestone structure aligned with funding rounds
- Flexibility for pivots and changes

**Investor Concerns**:
- Clean IP ownership
- Exclusive license in relevant field
- No burdensome ongoing obligations
- University support/endorsement
- Founder commitment and incentives
- Reasonable license economics (not >5% royalty drag)

**Balancing Act**:
- University wants appropriate return
- Investors want startup to succeed (not burdened by license)
- Terms should enable fundraising, not prevent it

### I-Corps and Commercialization Programs

**NSF Innovation Corps (I-Corps)**:
- Training program for commercialization
- $50K grant for customer discovery
- 7-week intensive program
- 100+ customer interviews
- Pivot or persevere decision
- Available at national and regional levels

**SBIR/STTR Programs**:
- Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR)
- Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR)
- Federal funding for startups ($50K-$1.5M+)
- Phase I: Feasibility ($50K-$250K; 6-12 months)
- Phase II: Development ($750K-$1.5M; 2 years)
- Phase III: Commercialization (non-SBIR funding)
- STTR requires university partnership (≥30% university)

## 5. Proof-of-Concept and De-Risking

### Valley of Death

**Challenge**: Gap between research results and commercial readiness
- TRL 3-4: Proof of concept in lab
- TRL 5-6: Prototype and testing needed
- TRL 7-8: Pilot/demonstration required
- **Gap**: Requires $100K-$2M+ for development; too risky for investors; beyond research grants

**Consequences**:
- Technologies stall at low TRL
- No licensees interested (too early)
- Startups fail to raise funding
- Societal benefits unrealized

### Proof-of-Concept Funding

**Sources**:
- University gap funds or proof-of-concept programs
- State economic development programs
- Foundation grants focused on commercialization
- SBIR/STTR Phase I
- Industry proof-of-concept partnerships
- Angel investors (for startups)

**Typical Uses**:
- Prototype development and testing
- Market research and customer discovery
- Validation studies or pilot trials
- Business model development
- Team building

**Best Practices**:
- Clear technical and commercial milestones
- Go/no-go decision points
- Leverage other resources (industry partnerships, shared facilities)
- Parallel development and business activities
- Stage investments (don't overfund too early)

### Commercialization Readiness

**Technical Readiness**:
- TRL 5-6 minimum for startup or license
- TRL 7-8 preferred
- Validated performance data
- Understood failure modes and limitations
- Manufacturability or scalability assessed

**Market Readiness**:
- Clear value proposition
- Target customer identified
- Market size estimated
- Competition understood
- Distribution channels identified
- Business model articulated

**IP Readiness**:
- Patent filed (provisional minimum; non-provisional preferred)
- Freedom to operate assessed
- Defensive publications for non-patented aspects
- Trade secrets identified and protected

**Team Readiness** (for startups):
- Entrepreneurial leadership identified
- Complementary team (technical, business, industry)
- Advisory board recruited
- Commitment to venture

## 6. Measuring Technology Transfer Impact

### TTO Performance Metrics

**Activity Metrics**:
- Invention disclosures received per year
- Patents filed; patents issued
- Licenses executed per year
- Startups formed per year

**Financial Metrics**:
- Licensing revenue (upfront + royalties + equity value)
- Revenue per research dollar (e.g., $1 licensing per $100 research)
- Revenue to cover TTO operations

**Impact Metrics**:
- Products commercialized
- Sales of licensed products
- Jobs created by licensees and startups
- Economic impact (GDP, tax revenue)
- Social and environmental benefits

**Benchmarking**:
- Compare to peer institutions
- AUTM (Association of University Technology Managers) annual survey
- Recognize wide variation by institution size and type

**Examples** (approximate for major research university):
- 150-200 invention disclosures/year
- 50-75 new patents filed/year
- 30-50 licenses executed/year
- 3-8 startups formed/year
- $10M-$50M licensing revenue/year

### Success Stories and Case Studies

**Documenting Impact**:
- Case studies of successful licenses
- Startup success stories
- Products in market
- Testimonials from licensees
- Economic impact studies

**Communication**:
- Annual technology transfer reports
- Feature stories on website and social media
- Press releases for major licenses or startups
- Presentations at industry and TTO conferences

## Key Takeaways

1. **TTO partnership essential**: Work closely with technology transfer office; early disclosure enables effective commercialization

2. **Multiple pathways exist**: Licensing, startups, open source—choose based on technology readiness, market, and resources

3. **Licensing requires patience**: Technology transfer typically takes years; persist and support TTO marketing efforts

4. **Startups are high-risk/high-reward**: Most fail but successful startups generate significant impact and potential returns

5. **Proof-of-concept critical**: Bridge "valley of death" with gap funding to increase commercial readiness and attractiveness

6. **Impact beyond revenue**: Technology transfer measured by societal benefits, not just licensing income
