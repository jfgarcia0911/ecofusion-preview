# Module 3: Legislative Process & Strategy

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:
- Navigate the federal and state legislative processes effectively
- Develop strategic legislative campaigns and identify key decision points
- Build relationships with legislators and staff
- Draft legislative language, amendments, and position statements
- Track legislation and gather legislative intelligence
- Identify and cultivate legislative champions
- Understand the role of committees, leadership, and procedural mechanisms

## 1. How a Bill Becomes Law: Federal Process

### Introduction and Sponsorship

**Bill Introduction**:
- Any member of Congress can introduce legislation
- House bills: H.R. 1, H.R. 2, etc.
- Senate bills: S. 1, S. 2, etc.
- Companion bills: Identical bills introduced in both chambers

**Finding Sponsors**:
- **Primary Sponsor**: Member who introduces the bill
- **Co-sponsors**: Members who add their support
- Building co-sponsor lists demonstrates support
- Bipartisan co-sponsors increase credibility

**Types of Legislation**:
- **Bills**: Become law if passed and signed (H.R., S.)
- **Joint Resolutions**: Similar to bills (H.J.Res., S.J.Res.)
- **Concurrent Resolutions**: Express congressional sentiment, no force of law
- **Simple Resolutions**: House or Senate only, procedural or symbolic

### Committee Referral and Consideration

**Referral Process**:
- Speaker (House) or Presiding Officer (Senate) refers to committee
- Parliamentarian provides guidance on jurisdiction
- Multiple committees if jurisdiction overlaps
- Sequential or split referral possible

**Agriculture Committee Jurisdiction**:
- Farm commodity programs
- Agricultural trade and promotion
- Rural development
- Food and nutrition programs
- Forestry (except wilderness)
- Agricultural credit and research
- Crop insurance

**Committee Process**:

1. **Subcommittee Referral** (often): Further specialization
2. **Hearings**: Testimony from witnesses, build record
3. **Markup**: Committee drafts and amends bill
4. **Committee Vote**: Approve, reject, or table
5. **Report**: Written explanation if approved, sent to floor

**Committee Power**:
- Chair controls agenda (majority party)
- Can block bills from consideration ("killing in committee")
- Can dramatically amend or rewrite
- Report language influences implementation

### Floor Consideration

**House Floor Process**:

**Rules Committee** (House only):
- Sets terms of debate for each bill
- Time limits, amendment procedures
- "Open rule": Amendments allowed
- "Closed rule": No amendments
- "Structured rule": Specific amendments only

**Floor Debate**:
- Time controlled by Rules Committee
- Majority and minority managers allocate time
- Members speak for or against
- Amendments offered and voted on

**Voting**:
- Voice vote, division (stand and be counted), recorded vote
- Passage requires majority of members voting
- 218 votes needed in House if all members present

**Senate Floor Process**:

**Unlimited Debate**:
- No Rules Committee equivalent
- Senators can speak indefinitely
- **Filibuster**: Extended debate to delay/prevent vote
- **Cloture**: 60 votes to end debate (Rule 22)

**Unanimous Consent**:
- Many bills pass without debate via UC agreement
- One objection can block
- Non-controversial bills via "hotline"

**Amendments**:
- Senators can offer amendments to any bill
- **Germaneness**: Amendments must relate to bill (House rule, not Senate)
- **Riders**: Unrelated provisions attached to must-pass bills

### Conference Committee

**Resolving Differences**:
- When House and Senate pass different versions
- Conference committee of both chambers appointed
- Negotiate compromise version
- **Conference Report**: Cannot be amended, up or down vote

**Alternative: Amendments Between Houses**:
- One chamber passes bill, sends to other
- Other chamber amends and sends back
- Ping-pong until agreement or conference

### Presidential Action

**Presidential Options**:
- **Sign**: Bill becomes law
- **Veto**: Reject and return with objections
- **Pocket Veto**: If Congress adjourns within 10 days, bill dies
- **Allow to Become Law**: No action for 10 days (Congress in session)

**Veto Override**:
- Requires 2/3 vote in both chambers
- Rare but possible with strong bipartisan support

**Enrolled Bill**:
- Final version printed on parchment
- Signed by Speaker and President Pro Tempore
- Sent to President

## 2. State Legislative Processes

### Variations Across States

**Structural Differences**:
- **Unicameral**: Nebraska only (one chamber)
- **Bicameral**: 49 states (House/Assembly and Senate)
- Session lengths: Year-round (CA, NY, PA) vs. part-time (many states)
- Partisan vs. non-partisan (NE)

**Process Similarities to Federal**:
- Bill introduction and sponsorship
- Committee referral and hearings
- Floor consideration and voting
- Governor signature or veto

**Key Differences**:
- Shorter timelines, faster pace
- Fewer staff, less specialization
- Citizen legislators (part-time)
- More direct lobbyist access
- Initiative and referendum (some states)

### Critical State-Level Differences

**Committee Systems**:
- Smaller committees, less specialization
- Joint committees more common
- Interim committees between sessions

**Floor Procedures**:
- Varied rules on debate and amendments
- Some states require multiple readings
- Crossover deadlines (bills must pass one chamber by date)

**Gubernatorial Powers**:
- **Line-Item Veto**: Veto parts of appropriations bills (most states)
- **Amendatory Veto**: Suggest changes (IL, VA, others)
- Varying override thresholds (2/3, 3/5, simple majority)

**Direct Democracy**:
- **Initiative**: Citizens petition to place measures on ballot
- **Referendum**: Legislative referral to voters
- **Recall**: Remove officials via petition and election
- 24 states have some form of initiative/referendum

## 3. Legislative Strategy Development

### Strategic Planning Framework

**1. Define Objectives**:
- **Proactive**: Advance new policy or program
- **Defensive**: Defeat harmful legislation
- **Amend**: Improve or modify pending legislation
- **Appropriate**: Secure funding for programs

**2. Assess the Landscape**:
- Current legislative session timeline
- Majority party priorities and leadership
- Committee composition and chairs
- Competing issues and legislative calendar
- Political environment and public opinion

**3. Identify Vehicles**:
- **Stand-alone Bill**: New legislation on specific issue
- **Amendment**: To existing bill under consideration
- **Omnibus Package**: Farm Bill, appropriations, tax bill
- **Authorization vs. Appropriation**: Policy law vs. funding

**4. Build Support**:
- Identify champions and sponsors
- Recruit co-sponsors
- Engage stakeholders and coalition partners
- Generate constituent contact
- Develop media and public affairs strategy

**5. Navigate Process**:
- Committee strategy and witness coordination
- Floor amendments and vote mobilization
- Conference committee engagement
- Presidential/gubernatorial outreach

### Timing and Legislative Calendar

**Two-Year Congressional Cycle**:
- Odd year (1st session): New Congress, major legislation
- Even year (2nd session): Shorter, election year, less ambitious
- All bills die at end of Congress unless enacted

**Annual Cycle**:
- January: Session begins, presidential State of Union
- Spring: Committee work, hearings, markups
- Summer: Floor consideration, appropriations push
- Fall: Final passage push, CR or omnibus if needed
- December: Lame duck session after elections (even years)

**Farm Bill Cycle**:
- Typically 5-year reauthorization
- 1-2 years of hearings and stakeholder input
- Committee development and markup
- Floor passage and conference
- Often extended temporarily if not completed

**State Legislative Calendars**:
- Vary widely by state
- Many states: January-May annual sessions
- Some states: Biennial sessions (every other year)
- Special sessions called by governor as needed

**Strategic Timing Considerations**:
- Introduce early to allow time for education and coalition building
- Attach to moving vehicles in final weeks
- Budget pressures in appropriations season
- Election-year politics affecting controversial bills

## 4. Building Legislative Champions

### Identifying Potential Champions

**Committee Leadership**:
- Chair and ranking member have greatest influence
- Subcommittee chairs for specialized issues
- "Cardinals" (Appropriations subcommittee chairs)

**Criteria for Champions**:
- Committee assignment aligned with issue
- District/state interests aligned
- Past voting record and positions
- Personal interest or expertise
- Leadership ambitions (looking for accomplishments)

**Agriculture Committee Members**:
- Members from agricultural districts
- Farm backgrounds or agricultural interests
- Leadership on committee

**Bipartisan Approach**:
- Seek champions from both parties
- Increases likelihood of success
- Demonstrates broad support
- Protects from partisan attacks

### Cultivating Champion Relationships

**Research and Preparation**:
- Know legislator's background, district, priorities
- Review voting record and past positions
- Understand district agriculture and economy
- Identify constituent connections

**Initial Outreach**:
- Request meeting through scheduler
- Provide brief overview of issue
- Explain constituent interest
- Often meet with senior staff first

**The Ask**:
- Be specific: Introduce bill, co-sponsor, vote yes, offer amendment
- Explain why it matters to their district/state
- Provide supporting materials
- Offer assistance and follow-up

**Ongoing Engagement**:
- Regular communication and updates
- Provide requested information promptly
- Thank publicly for support
- Invite to district events and site visits
- Long-term relationship building

### Working with Legislative Staff

**Types of Staff**:
- **Chief of Staff**: Manager, top advisor, gatekeeper
- **Legislative Director**: Oversees policy staff
- **Legislative Assistants (LAs)**: Handle specific issue areas
- **Legislative Correspondents**: Respond to constituent mail
- **Committee Staff**: Professional and clerical

**Staff Roles**:
- Advise members on policy and politics
- Draft legislation and amendments
- Negotiate with other offices
- Prepare members for hearings and floor speeches
- Track legislation and provide intelligence

**Effective Staff Engagement**:
- Respect their time and expertise
- Provide concise, accurate information
- Be responsive to questions and requests
- Build trust through reliability
- Understand they speak for the member

**Providing Value to Staff**:
- Expert testimony and analysis
- District-specific data and impacts
- Constituent stories and examples
- Draft legislative language
- Political intelligence (stakeholder positions)

## 5. Drafting Legislative Language

### Components of Legislation

**Bill Structure**:
1. **Short Title**: "This Act may be cited as..."
2. **Table of Contents**: For longer bills
3. **Findings**: Congressional purpose and context
4. **Purposes**: Objectives of the legislation
5. **Definitions**: Key terms used in the bill
6. **Substantive Provisions**: The actual policy changes
7. **Conforming Amendments**: Changes to existing law
8. **Authorization of Appropriations**: Funding levels
9. **Effective Date**: When law takes effect

**United States Code**:
- Codification of federal law
- Organized into 54 titles by subject
- Title 7: Agriculture
- Bills amend existing code sections

**Legislative Drafting Conventions**:
- Amend by striking and inserting
- Reference specific section, subsection, paragraph
- Precise legal language, defined terms
- Consistent formatting and structure

### Working with Legislative Counsel

**Office of Legislative Counsel**:
- House and Senate each have nonpartisan drafting office
- Professional drafters convert policy into legislative language
- Ensure legal accuracy and consistency
- Confidential service to members

**When to Use Legislative Counsel**:
- Formal bill introduction
- Complex amendments to existing law
- Technical corrections
- Final polishing of advocacy drafts

**Advocacy Organizations' Role**:
- Develop draft language to convey policy intent
- Provide to member's office as template
- Legislative counsel refines for introduction
- Track changes in committee and floor amendments

### Sample Legislative Provisions

**Example 1: Creating a New Program**

```
SEC. 999. CONTROLLED ENVIRONMENT AGRICULTURE RESEARCH GRANT PROGRAM.

(a) ESTABLISHMENT.—The Secretary shall establish a competitive grant program to support research on controlled environment agriculture production systems.

(b) ELIGIBLE ENTITIES.—The Secretary may make grants under this section to—
  (1) colleges and universities;
  (2) research institutions and organizations;
  (3) private sector entities; and
  (4) consortia of entities described in paragraphs (1) through (3).

(c) PRIORITY AREAS.—In making grants under this section, the Secretary shall give priority to research on—
  (1) energy efficiency and renewable energy integration;
  (2) crop varieties suited to controlled environments;
  (3) integrated pest management for protected culture;
  (4) water use efficiency and recycling; and
  (5) economic viability and market development.

(d) AUTHORIZATION OF APPROPRIATIONS.—There is authorized to be appropriated to carry out this section $10,000,000 for each of fiscal years 2025 through 2030.
```

**Example 2: Amending Existing Law**

```
SEC. 1234. INCLUSION OF CONTROLLED ENVIRONMENT AGRICULTURE IN SPECIALTY CROP BLOCK GRANT PROGRAM.

Section 101(i)(2) of the Specialty Crops Competitiveness Act of 2004 (7 U.S.C. 1621 note; Public Law 108-465) is amended—
  (1) by striking "and" at the end of subparagraph (G);
  (2) by redesignating subparagraph (H) as subparagraph (I); and
  (3) by inserting after subparagraph (G) the following:
    "(H) developing and implementing food safety practices for specialty crops produced in controlled environment agriculture systems; and".
```

### Position Statements and Bill Summaries

**One-Pager Format**:
- **Title and Bill Number**
- **Summary**: 2-3 sentence overview
- **Problem Statement**: What issue does this address?
- **Solution**: How does the bill solve it?
- **Support**: Who supports (organizations, stakeholders)
- **Talking Points**: 3-5 key messages
- **Contact**: Organization and person for questions

**Two-Page Bill Summary**:
- **Background**: Context and need for legislation
- **Key Provisions**: Section-by-section summary
- **Benefits**: Positive impacts and outcomes
- **State/District Impact**: Local relevance
- **Support and Opposition**: Stakeholder positions
- **Budget Impact**: CBO score or estimate

## 7. Committee Strategy

### Preparing for Committee Hearings

**Types of Hearings**:
- **Oversight**: Examine agency implementation of existing law
- **Legislative**: Consider specific bills
- **Investigative**: Examine problems or scandals
- **Nomination**: Confirm presidential appointees
- **Field Hearings**: Held in districts/states, not Capitol

**Witness Selection**:
- Members invite witnesses (majority more slots)
- Mix of: government officials, industry, advocacy groups, academics, affected individuals
- Testifiers submit written testimony in advance
- Oral testimony often 5 minutes, followed by Q&A

**Strategic Goals**:
- Build record supporting legislation
- Educate members and staff
- Generate media coverage
- Demonstrate stakeholder support
- Rebut opposition arguments

### Testifying Before Congress

**Preparation**:
- Written testimony (submitted 48 hours in advance typically)
- Oral summary (5 minutes or as specified)
- Anticipate questions and prepare responses
- Practice delivery and timing
- Bring supporting materials (charts, photos, samples)

**Written Testimony Best Practices**:
- Professional letterhead, contact information
- Address to "Chairman [Name], Ranking Member [Name], Members of the Committee"
- Introduce yourself and organization
- State key points upfront (executive summary)
- Provide evidence and examples
- Include specific policy recommendations
- Attach supporting materials as appendices
- Typically 5-10 pages plus appendices

**Oral Testimony Best Practices**:
- Thank Chairman and committee for opportunity
- Summarize 3-4 key points from written testimony
- Use stories and examples to illustrate
- Make eye contact with members
- Stay within time limit
- Close with specific call to action

**Question and Answer**:
- Listen carefully to full question
- Answer the question asked
- Be honest if you don't know ("I'll follow up")
- Avoid jargon and acronyms
- Respectful tone, even if question hostile
- Use opportunities to restate key messages

### Post-Hearing Follow-Up

**Questions for the Record (QFRs)**:
- Additional written questions from members
- Typically 10-14 day deadline for response
- Coordinate with coalition partners on responses
- Opportunity for additional advocacy

**Building on Hearing**:
- Thank members who asked helpful questions
- Provide additional materials requested
- Media outreach on testimony
- Share testimony with broader audiences
- Continue dialogue with staff

## 8. Floor Strategy and Vote Mobilization

### Amendment Strategy

**Offensive Amendments**:
- Improve bill with friendly amendments
- Demonstrate support through amendment votes
- Insert language that couldn't be included in committee
- Respond to developments since committee consideration

**Defensive Amendments**:
- Defeat harmful amendments
- Offer substitute amendments to blunt attacks
- Find middle ground to avoid worse amendments
- Protect against "gotcha" messaging amendments

**Amendment Drafting**:
- Work with member offices and legislative counsel
- Circulate to coalition for input
- Pre-file when required by rules
- Coordinate with floor managers

**Amendment Whipping**:
- Vote counting: Yes, No, Undecided, Unknown
- Target undecided members with personalized outreach
- Engage district stakeholders to contact members
- Work with leadership and whip organizations
- Real-time intelligence on vote switches

### Vote Counting and Mobilization

**Whip Count Process**:
- Start with expected support/opposition
- Identify persuadable middle
- Assign coalition partners to specific members
- Track commitments and report results
- Continue until confident of outcome

**Member Targeting**:
- **Champions**: Speaking on floor, rallying colleagues
- **Committed Yes**: Thank and ensure they vote
- **Leaning Yes**: Encourage and confirm
- **Undecided**: Intensive persuasion, district angle
- **Leaning No**: Long-shot persuasion or neutralization
- **Committed No**: Don't waste resources

**Message Discipline**:
- Consistent talking points across coalition
- Member-specific variations based on district
- Positive case for your position
- Rebuttals to opposition arguments
- Third-party validators and messengers

### Working with Leadership

**Majority Leadership**:
- **Speaker/Majority Leader**: Controls floor schedule
- **Majority Whip**: Counts votes, enforces party discipline
- **Committee Chairs**: Manage bills on floor

**Minority Leadership**:
- **Minority Leader**: Rallies opposition or seeks bipartisan deal
- **Minority Whip**: Counts minority votes
- **Ranking Members**: Lead minority opposition or support

**Leadership Engagement**:
- Understand leadership priorities
- Frame issue in terms of political benefits
- Demonstrate broad support in caucus
- Provide intelligence on vote counts
- Offer to mobilize outside support (business, advocacy groups)

## 9. Conference Committee Engagement

### The Conference Process

**Appointment of Conferees**:
- Leadership appoints conferees from each chamber
- Typically includes committee chairs, ranking members, subcommittee leaders
- Number varies; can be large (50+) on major bills

**Conference Negotiations**:
- Conferees meet to resolve differences
- Often in closed-door sessions
- Staff do much of detailed negotiation
- Leadership involvement on major issues

**Conference Report**:
- Single compromise version
- Cannot be amended
- Both chambers vote up or down
- Typically passes if agreement reached

### Strategic Positioning for Conference

**Strengthening House/Senate Position**:
- Strong vote margin on floor passage
- Unified caucus support
- Leadership commitment to priority provisions
- Outside stakeholder pressure

**Lobbying During Conference**:
- Engage conferees from both chambers
- Provide side-by-side comparisons of versions
- Offer compromise language
- Generate constituent and stakeholder contact
- Media strategy highlighting key issues

**Conference Report Advocacy**:
- Analyze final conference report quickly
- Decide: Support, oppose, or neutral
- Mobilize for final passage votes
- Prepare statements and media

## 10. Case Studies in Legislative Strategy

### Case Study 1: 2018 Farm Bill - Hemp Legalization

**Strategic Elements**:

**Champion Identification**:
- Senate Majority Leader Mitch McConnell (R-KY)
- Personal priority, Kentucky agriculture interest
- Maximum leverage as Majority Leader

**Coalition Building**:
- Kentucky farm groups and state officials
- Hemp industry and entrepreneurs
- Bipartisan support from members with hemp pilot programs
- Farm Bureau neutral to supportive

**Legislative Vehicle**:
- Senate Farm Bill as primary vehicle
- Alternative: Stand-alone bill, but farm bill more likely vehicle
- Appropriations rider as backup

**Committee Strategy**:
- Senate Agriculture Committee approved in markup
- House Agriculture initially resistant
- Educational campaign on hemp vs. marijuana

**Floor and Conference**:
- Strong Senate passage with hemp provision
- House version excluded hemp
- Conference negotiations: McConnell leverage prevailed
- Final bill included hemp legalization

**Outcome**: Hemp removed from controlled substances, USDA regulatory authority

**Lessons**:
- High-level champion crucial
- Farm Bill vehicle more feasible than stand-alone
- Education campaign over time built support
- Persistence through multiple Congresses

### Case Study 2: Specialty Crop Research Initiative

**Background**:
- Specialty crops historically underfunded in research
- Commodity crops dominated USDA research priorities
- Industry coalition sought dedicated funding

**Strategy**:

**Coalition Formation**:
- United Fresh Produce Association led
- Organic Trade Association, various commodity groups
- State departments of agriculture
- Land-grant universities conducting specialty crop research

**Legislative Development**:
- Developed specific program proposal with research priorities
- Identified funding level: $100M over 5 years initially
- Mandatory funding (not subject to annual appropriations)

**Member Engagement**:
- Targeted members from specialty crop states (CA, FL, WA, OR)
- Senate Agriculture Committee members especially
- Bipartisan outreach (specialty crops in red and blue states)

**Farm Bill Strategy**:
- Included in 2008 Farm Bill reauthorization
- Specialty Crop title provided political home
- Demonstrated broad specialty crop coalition support

**Appropriations Follow-Up**:
- Securing annual appropriations to reach authorized levels
- Competitive grant program administration by NIFA
- Demonstrating results to justify continued funding

**Expansion**:
- Each subsequent farm bill increased authorization
- 2018 Farm Bill: $80M/year in mandatory funding
- Organic Agriculture Research and Extension Initiative (OREI) created
- Model for other specialty programs

**Lessons**:
- Long-term strategy over multiple farm bills
- Coalition unity and persistence
- Mandatory funding provides stability
- Demonstrating results ensures continued support

## Summary

Effective legislative strategy requires understanding the formal processes, building relationships with key decision-makers, developing compelling policy proposals, and mobilizing support at critical moments.

**Key Success Factors**:
- **Champion Development**: Identify and cultivate legislative leaders
- **Strategic Timing**: Align with legislative calendar and policy windows
- **Coalition Building**: Unified voice amplifies influence
- **Quality Materials**: Professional, accurate legislative language and supporting documents
- **Relationship Building**: Long-term investment in members and staff
- **Vote Counting**: Rigorous tracking and targeted persuasion
- **Persistence**: Multi-year campaigns often needed for major policy changes

**Process Mastery**:
- Committee consideration is critical gatekeeper
- Floor amendments can dramatically change bills
- Conference committee determines final compromise
- Presidential/gubernatorial support or veto shapes outcomes

## Key Terms

- **Co-sponsor**: Member who adds their name in support of bill
- **Markup**: Committee session to amend and vote on bill
- **Filibuster**: Extended Senate debate to delay/prevent vote
- **Cloture**: 60-vote Senate threshold to end debate
- **Conference Committee**: Joint House-Senate committee to resolve differences
- **Unanimous Consent**: Senate procedure allowing action without objection
- **Whip Count**: Vote counting and member position tracking
- **Floor Manager**: Member managing bill during floor consideration
- **Rider**: Provision attached to unrelated bill
- **Germane**: Amendment relating to bill subject matter
