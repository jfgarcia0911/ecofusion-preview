# Module 13: Local & State Policy Engagement

## Learning Objectives

- Navigate state and local agricultural policy processes
- Understand zoning, land use, and right-to-farm laws
- Engage in state agricultural programs and incentives
- Build state-level advocacy capacity
- Address regional variations in agricultural policy

---

## 1. State Agricultural Policy Landscape

### State Departments of Agriculture

**Structure and Authority**:
- Cabinet-level or independent agencies
- Commissioner/Secretary (appointed or elected varies by state)
- Regulatory, promotional, and service functions
- State-specific priorities based on agricultural sectors

**Functions**:
- Agricultural marketing and promotion
- Plant and animal health programs
- Weights and measures
- Food safety inspection (state facilities)
- Agricultural statistics
- Farmland preservation
- Rural development

### State Legislatures

**Agricultural Committees**:
- Often combined with natural resources, rural affairs, food
- Vary in influence by state's agricultural importance
- State "farm bills" or agricultural policy acts

**Key Differences from Congress**:
- Part-time legislatures in many states
- Shorter sessions (many states only a few months)
- Fewer staff resources
- Greater legislator access
- Faster pace

**Regional Variations**:
- **Midwest**: Commodity crops, livestock focus
- **California**: Specialty crops, water policy
- **Southeast**: Diverse agriculture, forestry
- **Northeast**: Dairy, local food, farmland preservation
- **West**: Water, public lands, ranching

---

## 2. Zoning and Land Use

### Agricultural Zoning

**Purpose**: Preserve farmland, separate incompatible uses

**Zoning Categories**:
- Exclusive agricultural zones
- Minimum lot sizes (prevent subdivision)
- Restrictions on non-agricultural uses
- Special permits for farm-related businesses

**CEA Implications**:
- Is CEA "agriculture" under zoning?
- Industrial vs. agricultural classification
- Indoor operations in urban zones
- Accessory uses (retail, restaurants)

**Advocacy Needs**:
- Clarify CEA as agriculture in zoning codes
- Allow CEA in appropriate zones (urban, industrial, agricultural)
- Reduce barriers to entry
- Flexible regulations for innovation

### Urban Agriculture Zoning

**Traditional Zoning Barriers**:
- Agriculture not allowed in residential/commercial zones
- Size limitations (lot coverage, setbacks)
- Prohibition on sales
- Livestock restrictions

**Urban Ag Friendly Policies**:
- Expressly allow community gardens, urban farms
- Reduce or waive certain requirements
- Allow farm stands and sales
- Streamlined permitting

**Cities with Progressive Ordinances**:
- Seattle, Portland, Detroit, Cleveland, Philadelphia, San Francisco
- Models for other jurisdictions

### Right-to-Farm Laws

**Purpose**: Protect farms from nuisance lawsuits

**All 50 States**: Have some form of right-to-farm law

**Typical Provisions**:
- Agricultural operations not a nuisance if following best practices
- Protection if operation predates development
- Time limits (e.g., 1 year of operation)
- Exclusions for negligence or unsanitary conditions

**Debates**:
- CAFOs and environmental impacts
- Expanding development encroaching on farms
- Balance of property rights
- Application to CEA

## 3. State Agricultural Programs

### Marketing and Promotion

**Branding Programs**:
- "Jersey Fresh," "Pride of New York," "Florida Sunshine"
- State logos and certification
- Marketing campaigns
- Trade shows and export promotion

**State Commodity Commissions**:
- Similar to federal checkoff programs
- State assessments on production
- Research, promotion, education

**Buy Local Programs**:
- Local food promotion
- Farm-to-school, farm-to-institution
- State procurement preferences (some states)

### Agricultural Development and Incentives

**Tax Incentives**:
- Agricultural property tax assessment (based on ag use, not market value)
- Sales tax exemptions for farm equipment, inputs
- Income tax credits for beginning farmers, conservation, others

**Grants and Loans**:
- State agricultural development authorities
- Beginning farmer programs
- Value-added agriculture grants
- Agricultural infrastructure (processing, storage)

**Technical Assistance**:
- State extension services
- Business planning and consulting
- Food safety assistance

### Farmland Preservation

**Purchase of Development Rights (PDR)**:
- State buys development rights, land remains private agricultural use
- Permanent agricultural easements
- Funding varies (bond issues, taxes, general funds)
- States with significant programs: MD, PA, NJ, MA

**Agricultural Districts**:
- Voluntary enrollment for benefits
- Preferential taxation, right-to-farm protections
- Restrictions on eminent domain

**Transfer of Development Rights (TDR)**:
- Developers buy rights from farmland to develop elsewhere
- Market-based farmland preservation

## 4. Water and Natural Resources

### Water Rights and Allocation

**Western States (Prior Appropriation)**:
- "First in time, first in right"
- Water rights separate from land ownership
- Use it or lose it
- Transfers and markets

**Eastern States (Riparian Rights)**:
- Water rights tied to land ownership
- Reasonable use standard
- Permit systems in some states

**Groundwater**:
- Highly variable regulation by state
- Some states unregulated, others strict
- Depletion concerns (Ogallala Aquifer)

**CEA Considerations**:
- Water use efficiency advantages
- Permit requirements for withdrawal
- Discharge regulations
- Greywater and stormwater use

### State Environmental Regulation

**Delegated Federal Programs**:
- Clean Water Act (NPDES permits)
- Clean Air Act (state implementation plans)
- Pesticide regulation (state lead agencies)

**State-Specific Laws**:
- California environmental laws (CEQA, etc.)
- State endangered species acts
- Coastal zone management
- Groundwater protection

## 5. State Food Safety and Labeling

### State Inspections

**Meat and Poultry**:
- State inspection programs (at least equal to federal)
- Intrastate commerce only
- Cooperative agreements with USDA

**Other Foods**:
- State inspections of processors, retail
- Food code adoption (FDA model)
- Variation in requirements

### Cottage Food Laws

**Purpose**: Allow small-scale, home-based food production with reduced regulation

**Typical Provisions**:
- Non-potentially hazardous foods (baked goods, jams, etc.)
- Sales limits (e.g., $50,000/year)
- Labeling requirements
- Direct sales or limited retail

**Advocacy**: Reducing barriers for small producers, food entrepreneurs

### State Labeling and Claims

**State Laws**:
- Some states have specific labeling requirements beyond federal
- Example: California Prop 65 (cancer warnings)
- State organic programs (some states)

**Preemption Issues**:
- Federal law can preempt state laws
- National Bioengineered Food Disclosure preempted state GMO labeling

## 6. Energy and Climate Policy

### State Renewable Energy

**Renewable Portfolio Standards (RPS)**:
- Require percentage of electricity from renewable sources
- Opportunities for on-farm renewable energy (solar, wind, biogas)
- Renewable Energy Credits (RECs) tradable

**Net Metering**:
- Credit for electricity generated and sent to grid
- Supports farm-based solar and wind
- Policy varies by state

**Incentives and Rebates**:
- Tax credits for renewable energy
- Grants and low-interest loans
- PACE financing (Property Assessed Clean Energy)

**CEA Opportunities**:
- On-site solar (rooftop, parking canopy)
- Combined heat and power
- Reduce grid energy, lower carbon footprint

### State Climate Policies

**Cap-and-Trade Programs**:
- California, RGGI (Northeast states)
- Carbon pricing and markets
- Agricultural offsets potential

**Climate Action Plans**:
- State-level emissions reduction goals
- Agriculture sector strategies
- Adaptation and resilience

## 7. State Advocacy Strategies

### Building State Coalitions

**State Farm Bureau and Organizations**:
- Powerful in many states
- Grassroots membership
- Political influence

**State Commodity Groups**:
- State associations (dairy, grains, specialty crops)
- Alignment with national organizations

**Emerging Sector Coalitions**:
- Urban agriculture coalitions
- Organic and sustainable ag groups
- Food policy councils

### Engaging State Legislatures

**Access and Relationships**:
- Smaller, more accessible than Congress
- District connections critical
- Part-time legislators (many have other jobs)

**Lobbying Strategies**:
- In-district meetings and events
- Testify at committee hearings
- Provide draft legislation
- Coalition sign-on letters
- Grassroots mobilization

**Timing**:
- Know legislative session schedule
- Bill introduction deadlines
- Committee hearing schedules
- Fast pace, short sessions in many states

### Working with Governors

**Executive Authority**:
- Budget proposals
- Appointments (department heads, boards)
- Executive orders
- Veto power (line-item in most states)

**Engagement**:
- Transition teams for new governors
- Advisory councils and task forces
- State of the State addresses
- Budget advocacy

## 9. Regional Variations and Opportunities

### Northeast

**Characteristics**:
- Small farms, diverse agriculture
- Dairy challenges
- Local food movement strong
- Urban agriculture emerging

**Policy Priorities**:
- Farmland preservation (development pressure)
- Dairy stabilization programs
- Farm-to-school and institutions
- Climate adaptation

**CEA Opportunities**:
- Year-round production in cold climate
- Proximity to major metro areas
- Local food demand

### Southeast

**Characteristics**:
- Diverse agriculture (row crops, specialty, livestock, forestry)
- Rapid growth and development
- Climate and weather challenges (hurricanes, heat)

**Policy Priorities**:
- Water resources and quality
- Farmland protection
- Agricultural tourism
- Disaster assistance

**CEA Opportunities**:
- Hurricane resilience (controlled environment)
- Water efficiency
- Year-round production
- Urban markets (Atlanta, Charlotte, Miami)

### Midwest

**Characteristics**:
- Commodity agriculture dominance
- Large-scale operations
- Strong farm organizations
- Soil and water conservation

**Policy Priorities**:
- Commodity markets and prices
- Crop insurance
- Water quality (Gulf hypoxia)
- Biofuels

**CEA Opportunities**:
- Value-added local production
- Specialty crops in commodity region
- Urban food security (Chicago, Detroit)

### West

**Characteristics**:
- Specialty crops (fruits, vegetables, nuts, wine)
- Water scarcity
- Public lands grazing
- Diverse climates

**Policy Priorities**:
- Water allocation and rights
- Labor and immigration
- Exports and trade
- Environmental regulations

**CEA Opportunities**:
- Water efficiency critical
- Year-round specialty crops
- Urban agriculture (West Coast cities)

## 10. Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Seattle Urban Agriculture Zoning

**Background**:
- Traditional zoning prohibited agriculture in most zones
- Growing interest in urban farming

**Policy Changes** (2010-2012):
- Allowed urban farms and community gardens in most zones
- Reduced parking and landscaping requirements
- Allowed retail sales of farm products
- Clarified livestock and beekeeping rules

**Process**:
- Community advocacy and task force
- City planning department engagement
- Public hearings and comment
- City Council adoption

**Results**:
- Significant increase in urban farms
- Economic development and job creation
- Model for other cities

### Case Study 2: State Agricultural Water Efficiency Incentives

**California Example**:
- Drought and water scarcity
- Agricultural water use (80% of human use)
- Efficiency incentives

**Programs**:
- Grants for drip irrigation, precision application
- Tax incentives for water-saving technology
- Technical assistance
- Water banking and markets

**Outcomes**:
- Increased efficiency adoption
- Water savings and crop value maintained
- Model for water-scarce regions

**CEA Connection**:
- Indoor agriculture water efficiency
- Potential for incentives
- Climate resilience

## Summary

State and local policy profoundly affects agriculture, from zoning and water rights to taxation and environmental regulation. Effective advocacy requires understanding diverse state legal frameworks, building local coalitions, and engaging accessible state and local officials.

**Key Strategies**:
- Engage early in state legislative sessions
- Build coalitions with state farm organizations
- Provide local examples and economic impacts
- Work with state departments of agriculture
- Address zoning and land use barriers for CEA
- Leverage state incentive programs

---

## Key Terms

- **Right-to-Farm Law**: State law protecting farms from nuisance suits
- **Agricultural Zoning**: Land use regulation to preserve farmland
- **PDR**: Purchase of Development Rights (farmland preservation)
- **Cottage Food Law**: Reduced regulation for small-scale home food production
- **Prior Appropriation**: Western water rights system
- **Riparian Rights**: Eastern water rights tied to land
- **RPS**: Renewable Portfolio Standard
- **Net Metering**: Credit for electricity sent to grid
- **Food Policy Council**: Multi-stakeholder group on food system policy
