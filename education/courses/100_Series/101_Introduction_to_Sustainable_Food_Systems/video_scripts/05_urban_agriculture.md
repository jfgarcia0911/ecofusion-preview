## Script

[ON SCREEN: "Module 5 - Urban Agriculture & Food Security"]

Here's a number that shapes the future of food. By **2050**, the United Nations expects around **68 percent** of the world's people to live in cities. More than two in every three of us. And cities, for the most part, don't grow their own food. They import it - which is exactly the long, fragile supply chain we met in Module 1.

So a growing movement is asking: what if cities fed themselves, at least in part?

[ON SCREEN: A montage - a window box, a vacant-lot garden, a rooftop farm, a warehouse of stacked greens]

That's **urban agriculture**: growing food **within or around cities**. It comes in every size. A window box of herbs. A **community garden** - a **shared growing space managed by the people who use it**. **Rooftop farms**, turning unused roofs into productive fields. And **vertical farms** - crops grown in stacked layers indoors, which **maximise production in a very small footprint**. When land is scarce and expensive, you grow up instead of out.

[ON SCREEN: A city map with a highlighted neighbourhood and no grocery icons in it]

Why does it matter? Two words: **food security**. Food security exists when all people, at all times, have access to enough safe, nutritious food. Four things have to hold: food must be **available**, people must be able to **access** it, their bodies must be able to **use** it well - which means nutritious food - and all of that has to be **stable** over time.

Many cities fail on access. They have what's called a **food desert** - a neighbourhood with **little access to affordable, nutritious food**. There might be a fast-food outlet and a convenience store on every corner, but no grocery selling fresh vegetables within walking distance. For a family without a car, that's a real barrier.

[ON SCREEN: Fresh produce changing hands at a neighbourhood farm stand]

Urban agriculture helps by putting **local, accessible fresh food** right where people live - and a city that grows some of its own food is less exposed when long supply chains break down, as many did during recent crises.

The benefits go beyond food: greener neighbourhoods, cooler streets, less food transport, new jobs, and neighbours who get to know each other over a shared garden bed.

[ON SCREEN: A soil test kit; a "for sale" sign on a city lot]

But let's be honest about the challenges. **Land is expensive** in cities. **Soil can be contaminated** by past industry - old factory sites can leave lead and other toxins in the ground, which is why many urban growers test their soil or grow in raised beds with clean soil. And one thing people often get wrong: urban farming usually has **higher labour costs**, not lower. City wages are higher, and small plots can't use the big machines rural farms rely on.

[ON SCREEN: A council chamber - "Zoning"]

Policy matters too. Many cities once banned farming inside city limits by accident, through old rules. The single most helpful change is **zoning** that explicitly **allows agricultural use in urban areas** - plus rules that permit composting, beekeeping, and selling produce at the farm gate.

[ON SCREEN: Four city postcards - Havana, Detroit, Singapore, Paris]

And it works. After the Soviet Union collapsed in the 1990s, Cuba lost its food and fuel imports almost overnight. **Havana** responded with thousands of urban organic gardens called *organopónicos*, which grew a large share of the city's fresh vegetables. **Detroit** turned vacant land into hundreds of urban farms and community gardens. **Singapore**, with almost no farmland at all, is betting on high-tech vertical farms. And **Paris** has encouraged farms on rooftops across the city.

[ON SCREEN: Instructor, full frame]

Different cities, different reasons - necessity, opportunity, ambition - but the same idea. Food doesn't have to come from far away.

Read the Module 5 lesson, then take Quiz 5.

## Figures to verify before rendering

- **68% by 2050** - UN DESA's 2018 projection. Solid.
- **Havana** - the lesson says organopónicos provide "50-70% of the city's fresh produce". That figure is often repeated but hard to source, so the script says "a large share" instead. Put the number back only if you can source it.
- **Paris** - the lesson says its goal of 100 hectares of urban agriculture by 2020 was "achieved". Reports suggest it fell short, so the script just says Paris "encouraged farms on rooftops across the city". Don't state the 100 hectares as achieved without checking.
- **Four pillars of food security** - availability, access, utilisation and stability, as the FAO defines them. The lesson's diagram shows the first two clearly; the script names all four.
