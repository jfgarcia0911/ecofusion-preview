## Script

[ON SCREEN: "Module 6 - Controlled Environment Agriculture"]

For ten thousand years, farmers have been at the mercy of the weather. A late frost, a drought, a hailstorm - and a season's work is gone. **Controlled Environment Agriculture** - **CEA** for short - is the idea of taking the weather out of the equation. Instead of adapting your crops to the conditions, you build the conditions your crops need.

[ON SCREEN: A line of structures from simple to complex - cold frame, row cover, low tunnel, high tunnel, greenhouse, vertical farm]

CEA isn't one thing; it's a spectrum. At the simple end there's a **cold frame** - a box with a clear lid that traps the sun's warmth. Then fabric **row covers**, then plastic **tunnels** over hoops, then walk-in **high tunnels**. Further along is the **greenhouse** - and a **greenhouse with climate control** is the classic example of CEA. At the far end is the **vertical farm**: an indoor space with no sunlight at all, where every condition is engineered, and crops are grown in **stacked layers** to fit as much as possible into a small space.

The further along the spectrum you go, the more control you get - and the more it costs.

[ON SCREEN: Six icons - light, temperature, humidity, CO2, water and nutrients, air movement]

So what exactly are we controlling? Six things. **Light** - how bright, what colour, how many hours a day. **Temperature**. **Humidity**. **Carbon dioxide** - plants breathe it in, and adding a little more can speed their growth. **Water and nutrients**, delivered straight to the roots. And **air movement**, to keep plants healthy and disease away.

[ON SCREEN: Rows of lettuce under pink-purple light - "LED grow lights"]

The technology that made modern indoor farming practical is the **LED grow light**. LEDs use far less energy than older lamps, run cooler, and can be tuned to the exact colours of light plants use most.

[ON SCREEN: Three benefit cards - "Year-round", "90%+ less water", "Few or no pesticides"]

The advantages are big. The first is the **primary** one: **year-round production with full control** of conditions. Lettuce in December, wherever you are.

Second, **water**. Most CEA systems **recirculate** their water instead of letting it soak away into the ground, so they can use **90 percent less water**, or more, than growing the same crop in a field.

Third, **pests**. An enclosed space keeps most pests out to begin with, so CEA growers can use **far fewer pesticides** - often none at all.

[ON SCREEN: An electricity meter spinning fast]

Now the catch. The biggest challenge is **energy**. Lights, heating, cooling, fans - it adds up, and electricity is often a vertical farm's largest bill. That's also why CEA's environmental case depends heavily on *where* its energy comes from. A vertical farm running on renewable power is a very different thing from one running on coal.

[ON SCREEN: Basil, microgreens, lettuce, strawberries]

The high costs also shape what gets grown. The crops that pay their way are **leafy greens, herbs, and specialty crops** - fast-growing, high-value plants like lettuce, basil, and microgreens. You won't see indoor wheat fields any time soon; the economics don't work.

[ON SCREEN: Instructor, full frame]

CEA won't replace outdoor farming. But for fresh, perishable food grown close to where people live - especially in the cities we met in the last module - it's one of the most important tools we have.
