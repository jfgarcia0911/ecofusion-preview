## Script

[ON SCREEN: "Module 1 - The Global Food System"]

Every day, roughly eight billion people need to eat. The machinery that makes that happen is called the **food system** - and it's worth knowing exactly what that means, because we'll use the idea all course long.

[ON SCREEN: Five words appear in a loop - Production, Processing, Distribution, Consumption, Waste management]

A food system has five parts. **Production** - growing crops and raising animals. **Processing** - turning raw harvest into food, from washing lettuce to baking bread. **Distribution** - moving it all, by truck, ship, and rail, to stores and restaurants. **Consumption** - how we buy, cook, and eat it. And the part people forget: **waste management** - what happens to everything we don't eat.

Production, processing, distribution, consumption, waste. Hold on to those five.

[ON SCREEN: "About 1 in 4 of the world's workers" - an icon of a farmer]

The scale is enormous. Around a billion people work in agriculture - roughly a quarter of the world's workforce. And it's thirsty work. About **70 percent** of all the fresh water humans withdraw goes to agriculture. More than industry. More than every home and city combined.

[ON SCREEN: Three grains - wheat, rice, corn - "About 60% of the world's calories"]

It's also surprisingly narrow. Just three crops - wheat, rice, and corn - provide around 60 percent of the calories the world eats. That's a lot of weight resting on very few plants, and in Module 2 we'll see why that's risky.

[ON SCREEN: A map with a line tracing a tomato from Mexico to a US kitchen]

Now let's follow the food. **Food miles** means the distance food travels from where it's grown to where it's eaten. An often-quoted estimate says the typical food item in the US travels around **1,500 miles** to reach you. Picture a supermarket tomato: grown in Mexico, picked green so it survives the trip, trucked across the border, ripened in a warehouse, and finally shelved near you - two or three weeks after it left the plant.

[ON SCREEN: Bar chart - beef at the top, vegetables near the bottom]

Distance is only part of the story, though. What really drives the climate impact of food is *what* it is. That's measured as a **carbon footprint** - all the greenhouse gases released growing, processing, moving, and disposing of it. By the figures we use in this course, beef comes in around 27 kilograms of carbon dioxide for every kilogram of meat. Vegetables? About 2. Plant foods sit at the bottom of the chart; red meat at the top.

Water tells the same story. A single pound of beef takes roughly **1,800 gallons** of water to produce - far more than any other common food.

[ON SCREEN: One plate out of three greyed out - "About 1/3 of food is lost or wasted"]

And here's the number that stops most people. About **a third** of all food produced for people is lost or wasted - over a billion tons a year. All that water, soil, and energy, spent on food nobody eats.

[ON SCREEN: Two columns - "Global" and "Local"]

So is the answer simply to eat local? Local food systems have real benefits: fresher food, money that stays in the community, and you can meet the person who grew it. One popular model is the **CSA - Community Supported Agriculture** - where you buy a share of a farm's harvest up front and receive a box through the season.

But be honest about the trade-offs too. Local means seasonal. You won't get strawberries in January. A global system gives us year-round variety; a local one gives us freshness and connection. Neither is simply right - and learning to weigh that is what this course is about.

[ON SCREEN: Instructor, full frame]

Before you move on, try this. Think of one meal you ate this week and ask of each ingredient: where was this grown? You'll find an activity for exactly that - **Map Your Meal** - under Course resources.

[PAUSE 2 seconds]

Then read the Module 1 lesson for the full detail, and take Quiz 1. See you in Module 2.

## Figures to verify before rendering

An avatar states every number with complete confidence. Fixing one afterwards means re-rendering.

- **1,500 food miles** - a widely repeated but decades-old US estimate. The script labels it "an often-quoted estimate"; keep that framing.
- **Beef ~27 kg CO2e per kg** - this matches the course's source table, which is an older lifecycle study. Newer research puts beef considerably higher. Fine to teach as "by the figures we use in this course".
- **Vegetables lowest** - the Module 1 lesson table actually lists *fruits* (1.1) below vegetables (2.0), while Quiz 1 says vegetables. The script says "plant foods sit at the bottom" to stay true to both. Worth fixing the quiz or the table.
- **~60% of calories from three crops** - commonly cited; some sources say closer to half.
- **1,800 gallons per pound of beef** - consistent with Water Footprint Network averages.
- **Deliberately left out** - the lesson's US spending split (54% grocery, 37% restaurants) is out of date: USDA data shows Americans now spend more on food away from home. The lesson's "0.5 kg CO2 per tomato" also looks high for one tomato. Neither is tested, so neither is spoken.
