**SCRIPT**

So what exactly is a food system? A food system is all the processes, infrastructure, and people involved in feeding a population. If it touches food on its way to being eaten, or wasted, it's part of the food system.

## Scene 3 — The five parts

**SCRIPT**
```
A food system has five main parts. First, production: growing crops and raising animals. Second, processing: turning raw materials into food products. Third, distribution: moving food from the people who produce it to the people who eat it. Fourth, consumption: how food is bought, prepared, and eaten. And fifth, the part people forget: waste management, handling food waste and everything left over. Production, processing, distribution, consumption, and waste management. Hold on to those five. The rest of this module walks through each one.
```

## Scene 4 — It's a cycle, not a line

**SCRIPT**
```
It helps to picture the food system as a cycle rather than a straight line. It starts with inputs: seeds, water, energy, labor, and land. Those inputs feed production, on farms, ranches, and fisheries. From there, food goes to processing and packaging, then to distribution and retail, and finally to consumption. And whatever isn't eaten flows into waste management, which, in a well-designed system, feeds back into the start again.
```

Inputs → Production → Processing → Distribution → Consumption → Waste → back to Inputs

**SCRIPT**

The scale of all this is hard to picture. The world produces around ten billion tons of food every year. That's enough to feed everyone on the planet. ## Scene 9 — Part 2: Processing

**SCRIPT**
```
Next comes processing: everything that happens between the farm and your table. Processing comes in four levels. Minimal processing is just cleaning, sorting, and packaging, like bagged salad or fresh fruit. Moderate processing means cutting, freezing, or canning, like frozen vegetables or canned beans. Extensive processing physically or chemically transforms the food, like turning grain into bread, milk into cheese, or seeds into cooking oil. And ultra-processed foods are industrial formulations, such as chips, sodas, and ready meals, often made mostly from ingredients you'd never cook with at home.
```

## Scene 10 — Part 3: Distribution

**SCRIPT**
```
Third is distribution: how food actually reaches you. In the conventional system, food passes through many hands. It starts with the farm or producer. From there it goes to an aggregator or packer, who gathers produce from many farms. Then to a processor or manufacturer. Then to a distributor or wholesaler. And from the wholesaler it splits three ways: to grocery stores, to restaurants and food service, and to institutions like schools and hospitals. Only then does it reach you. Every one of those steps adds time, distance, and cost.

## Scene 11 — Part 4: Consumption

**SCRIPT**
```
Fourth is consumption: how people get and eat their food. In the United States, there's been a big shift. For most of history, most food money was spent at grocery stores. Today it's the other way round. According to the US Department of Agriculture, in 2024 about fifty-nine cents of every food dollar was spent eating out, at restaurants, cafes, and takeaways, and only about forty-one cents on groceries to eat at home. Buying directly from farms, through farmers markets or farm boxes, is still only a tiny slice.
```

## Scene 12 — Part 5: Waste management

**SCRIPT**
```
And fifth, waste management: what happens to food nobody eats. Worldwide, about a third of all food produced is lost or wasted. That's around one point three billion tons every year. That's a problem, because food rotting in a landfill releases methane, a powerful greenhouse gas. Only about five percent is composted, where it could have become fresh soil instead.

## Scene 13 — Food miles

**SCRIPT**
```
Now let's follow food on its journey from the farm to your fork. The first idea to know is food miles: the distance food travels from where it's grown to where it's eaten. The distances vary enormously. An often-quoted estimate says conventional fresh produce in the United States travels around fifteen hundred miles. Imported produce can travel more than five thousand miles, by ship or even by plane. Processed foods travel around thirteen hundred miles. Local produce, under one hundred. And food from your own garden? Zero miles. Just a walk outside.
```

## Scene 14 — The journey of a tomato

**SCRIPT**
```
Let's make that real by following a single supermarket tomato. The seed was produced in the Netherlands and shipped to a farm in Mexico. There, the plant grows for six to eight weeks. The tomato is picked while it's still green, so it survives the trip. At a packing house, it's sorted, boxed, and cooled. It crosses the border into Texas for inspection and paperwork, then travels to a regional distribution center, where it's stored and ripened with a gas called ethylene. Only then does it reach your local grocery store, and finally your kitchen. That's around fifteen hundred miles and two to three weeks. And if it isn't eaten, one more stop: the landfill.
```

## Scene 16 — Which foods have the biggest footprint?

**SCRIPT**
```
Here's how common foods compare, using one widely used study. The figures are kilograms of carbon dioxide released for every kilogram of food. Beef is the highest, at around twenty-seven. Cheese, about thirteen and a half. Pork, about twelve. Chicken, about seven. Eggs, about five. Rice, under three. Vegetables, about two. And fruits, the lowest, at about one. The pattern is clear: animal foods, especially red meat, sit at the top, and plant foods sit at the bottom.

## Scene 17 — Global vs local

**SCRIPT**
```
So how does a global food system compare with a local one? Let's line them up. Distance: global food travels a thousand miles or more, local food under a hundred. Freshness: global food is days or weeks old, local food hours or days. Variety: the global system gives year-round availability, while local food is seasonal. Price: global food is often cheaper thanks to its huge scale, while local prices vary. Traceability: global supply chains are complex, local ones direct. Money: the global system mostly benefits large corporations, the local one supports local businesses. Environment: global food means more transport emissions, local food less, although production methods matter too. And relationships: global food is anonymous, while with local food, you can often meet the person who grew it.
```
**CANVAS** — Text, two columns:

                GLOBAL              LOCAL

Price           often lower         variable
Traceability    complex             direct
Money goes to   corporations        local businesses
Relationship    anonymous           personal
```

## Scene 19 — Benefits of local food

**SCRIPT**
```
Why do people choose local? There are seven big benefits. Local food is often fresher and more nutritious. It supports the local economy. It builds connections within a community. It reduces transport emissions. It helps preserve nearby farmland. It increases food security, because a community isn't depending entirely on faraway suppliers. And it promotes biodiversity, because small local farms tend to grow a wider range of crops.
```

## Scene 20 — The challenges, and the balance

**SCRIPT**
```
But local food has real challenges too, and it's important to be honest about them. It's limited by the seasons: you won't find local strawberries in January in most places. It sometimes costs more. There's less variety. It asks people to change their shopping habits. There are gaps in the infrastructure needed to get local food to market. And it's hard to scale up. So a global system gives us year-round variety and lower prices, and a local one gives us freshness and connection. Neither one is simply right. Learning to weigh those trade-offs is what this whole course is about.
```

## Scene 21 — Key takeaways and vocabulary

**SCRIPT**

Let's pull it all together. One: a food system includes production, processing, distribution, consumption, and waste management. Two: in the conventional system, food travels around fifteen hundred miles. Three: carbon footprint varies hugely by food type, from around twenty-seven kilograms for beef to about two for vegetables. Four: local food systems offer real benefits, but they come with trade-offs. And five: understanding the system is the first step toward improving it. ## Notes for the course team (don't paste these into Synthesia)

**Quiz 1 is fully covered.** All ten questions are taught in this video, including three that the Module 1 lesson itself doesn't teach: 70% of fresh water (Scene 5), 60% of calories from three crops (Scene 6), and beef's water footprint (Scene 16).

**Where this video differs from the written lesson**, because the lesson's figures were out of date or couldn't be checked:

| Topic | Written lesson says | Video says | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| US food spending | 54% grocery, 37% restaurants | about 59% eating out, 41% groceries | USDA Economic Research Service, 2024 |
| Where food waste goes | Landfill 58%, compost 6%, and others | about 60% landfill, about 5% compost | US EPA, 2019 |
| Agriculture's size | "$5+ trillion industry" | "about 4% of the world economy" | the dollar figure couldn't be confirmed, so it's left out |
| The tomato | "~0.5 kg CO2 per tomato" | not mentioned | looks too high for one tomato |
| Food miles | 1,500 miles | "an often-quoted estimate" of 1,500 | a decades-old figure, so it's framed as an estimate |
| Carbon by food | table of figures | "using one widely used study" | an older study; newer research puts beef higher |

The written lesson should be updated to match, so a learner who reads it after watching doesn't meet two different sets of numbers.
