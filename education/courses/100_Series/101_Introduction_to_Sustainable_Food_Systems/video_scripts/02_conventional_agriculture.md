## Script

[ON SCREEN: "Module 2 - Challenges in Conventional Agriculture"]

Let me be clear about something before we start. Modern farming is an extraordinary achievement. It feeds more people than at any time in history. This module isn't about blaming farmers. It's about the bill that comes with the way we farm - a bill that's quietly coming due.

We'll look at it in four parts: soil, water, climate, and people.

[ON SCREEN: A cross-section of soil - "1 inch = 500 to 1,000 years"]

**Soil** first. About a **third** of the world's soil is already degraded - eroded, compacted, or stripped of nutrients. That matters because soil is, for practical purposes, not renewable. It takes somewhere between **500 and 1,000 years** to form a single inch of topsoil. We can lose that inch in a few seasons of bare, ploughed ground and heavy rain.

[ON SCREEN: A satellite image sequence - the Aral Sea shrinking]

**Water** next. Remember from Module 1: agriculture takes 70 percent of our fresh water. Here's what that can look like at its worst. The **Aral Sea**, in Central Asia, was once the fourth-largest lake on Earth. From the 1960s, the rivers that fed it were diverted to irrigate cotton fields. Within a few decades, most of the sea was gone - leaving a salt desert, collapsed fishing towns, and dust storms carrying pesticide residue. It's one of the worst environmental disasters of the last century, and it was caused by irrigation.

[ON SCREEN: A river mouth with a green bloom spreading into the sea]

Water isn't only overused - it's polluted. When fertilizer washes off fields, the nitrogen and phosphorus feed huge blooms of algae. That process is called **eutrophication**. When the algae die and rot, they use up the oxygen in the water. The result is a **dead zone** - an area of water with too little oxygen to support life. Fish leave or die. The Gulf of Mexico has one of the largest, fed by fertilizer from across the American Midwest.

[ON SCREEN: A pie chart - "Food system: about 26% of greenhouse gas emissions"]

Then there's the **climate**. The food system produces around **26 percent** of all human greenhouse gas emissions - from clearing land, from livestock, from fertilizer, from transport and waste. And one gas deserves a special mention: **nitrous oxide**, released mainly from fertilizer and manure. Molecule for molecule, it traps about **265 times** as much heat as carbon dioxide.

[ON SCREEN: "10 calories of fossil fuel → 1 calorie of food"]

Industrial farming also runs on fossil fuel - for tractors, for fertilizer, for pumping water, for shipping. By one widely cited estimate, the industrial system spends around **ten calories of fossil energy for every one calorie of food** it delivers to your plate.

[ON SCREEN: Many varieties of apples fading to three]

We've also lost variety. Around **75 percent** of crop diversity has disappeared in the last hundred years, as farms narrowed down to a handful of high-yield varieties. Less diversity means less resilience. When a disease arrives that one variety can't resist, there's nothing to fall back on.

[ON SCREEN: A dollar bill - "1950s: 41 cents to the farmer" becomes "Today: about 14 cents"]

Finally, **people**. In the 1950s, a farmer received around **41 cents** of every dollar spent on food. Today it's about **14 cents**. The rest goes to processing, packaging, transport, marketing, and retail. Many farmers are working harder than ever for a shrinking share - and many farmworkers earn very little.

[ON SCREEN: Instructor, full frame]

So is industrial farming simply wrong? It's not that simple. It produces huge amounts of food cheaply, and we can't switch it off overnight. But when soil takes centuries to form and we lose it in years, when a sea can vanish, the current way isn't one we can keep up forever. The question isn't *whether* things change. It's how - and that's where we go next.

Read the Module 2 lesson, then take Quiz 2.

## Figures to verify before rendering

- **26% of emissions** - from a widely used 2018 study. Other credible estimates run higher (roughly a third) depending on what's counted. "Around 26 percent" is defensible.
- **265 times CO2** - the IPCC's earlier (AR5) figure. The latest assessment says about 273. Either is fine; 265 matches the quiz.
- **10:1 fossil-energy ratio** - an often-repeated figure that simplifies a contested calculation. The script says "by one widely cited estimate"; keep that.
- **75% crop diversity lost** - FAO's frequently quoted figure for loss since 1900.
- **Farmer's share 41 cents → ~14 cents** - consistent with USDA food-dollar data, though the two figures come from different measurement series.
- **Gulf of Mexico dead zone** - an example added for the video, not in the lesson. Well documented, but check you're happy to name it.
