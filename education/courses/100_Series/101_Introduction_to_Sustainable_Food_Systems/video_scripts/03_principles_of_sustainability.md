## Script

[ON SCREEN: "Module 3 - Principles of Sustainability"]

"Sustainable" is on everything now - coffee cups, shampoo, airline tickets. When a word is used that often, it can stop meaning anything. So in this module, we're going to pin it down.

[ON SCREEN: "Meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."]

The best-known definition is this: sustainability means **meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.** For farming, that means growing the food we need today in a way that leaves the soil, the water, and the climate able to feed people tomorrow.

[ON SCREEN: Three overlapping circles - Environmental, Social, Economic]

To make that practical, we use **three pillars**. **Environmental** - does it protect soil, water, air, and wildlife? **Social** - is it fair to farmers, workers, and communities? **Economic** - can it actually pay its way? You'll also hear this called the **triple bottom line**: people, planet, profit.

Here's the key idea. You need all three. A farm that's wonderful for the environment but goes bankrupt isn't sustainable. Neither is a profitable farm that exhausts its soil, or one that only works because its workers are underpaid. Sustainability lives where the three circles overlap.

[ON SCREEN: A field with a green cover crop between rows]

So what does it look like in the field? Let's go through the core practices.

**Cover crops and no-till farming.** Bare soil washes and blows away. Cover crops - plants grown between harvests - hold soil in place. No-till means planting without ploughing, so the soil's structure stays intact. Together, they're among the best defences against erosion.

[ON SCREEN: A four-field diagram, crops rotating each season]

**Crop rotation.** Growing the same crop in the same place year after year lets its pests and diseases build up, and drains the same nutrients. Rotating crops breaks those cycles - pests lose their host - and different crops balance what they take from and give back to the soil.

**Biodiversity.** Growing many species and varieties, not one. A diverse farm is a resilient farm, for the same reason a diverse investment portfolio is safer.

[ON SCREEN: Ladybugs on a leaf - "Integrated Pest Management"]

**Integrated pest management**, or **IPM**. The goal isn't to kill every pest. It's to keep pest damage low using several methods together - beneficial insects, crop rotation, physical barriers - and to use chemicals only as a last resort, choosing the least harmful option.

[ON SCREEN: Drip irrigation lines at the base of plants; a rain barrel]

**Smart water use.** Drip irrigation puts water right at the roots instead of spraying it into the air, and rainwater harvesting captures what falls for free.

[ON SCREEN: A loop - food scraps → compost → soil → food]

Now two bigger ideas. The first is the **circular economy**. In nature, there's no such thing as waste - everything becomes food for something else. A circular food system copies that: one process's waste becomes another's input. Food scraps become compost; compost feeds soil; soil grows food.

The second is **regenerative agriculture**. Sustainable means *not making things worse*. Regenerative goes further: it aims to actively **improve and restore** soil health over time, leaving the land better than you found it.

[ON SCREEN: "Food sovereignty"]

And one more term you'll meet: **food sovereignty** - the right of people to define their own food systems, instead of having them dictated from outside.

[ON SCREEN: Instructor, full frame]

If you remember one thing from this module, make it the three circles. Every method we look at next - organic, permaculture, urban farms, greenhouses - you can test against them. Is it good for the planet? Fair to people? Can it pay its way?
