### 6.1 Approaching Ethical Case Studies

#### Framework for Analysis

```
ETHICAL CASE ANALYSIS PROCESS

1. UNDERSTAND THE SITUATION
   ├─ What are the facts?
   ├─ What is uncertain?
   └─ What is the core ethical question?

2. IDENTIFY STAKEHOLDERS
   ├─ Who is affected?
   ├─ What are their interests?
   └─ Who has power? Who is vulnerable?

3. CLARIFY VALUES IN CONFLICT
   ├─ What values are at stake?
   ├─ Where do they conflict?
   └─ What is being prioritized?

4. CONSIDER ALTERNATIVES
   ├─ What are the options?
   ├─ What are consequences of each?
   └─ Are there creative solutions?

5. APPLY ETHICAL FRAMEWORKS
   ├─ Consequentialist view?
   ├─ Deontological view?
   ├─ Virtue ethics view?
   ├─ Care ethics view?
   └─ Environmental ethics view?

6. MAKE A DECISION
   ├─ What do you choose?
   ├─ Why is it most ethical?
   └─ What are you willing to sacrifice?

7. REFLECT ON IMPLICATIONS
   ├─ What precedent does this set?
   ├─ What are long-term effects?
   └─ What did you learn?
```

### 6.2 Case Study 1: The GMO Dilemma

#### Scenario

**Golden Rice** is a genetically modified rice engineered to produce beta-carotene (vitamin A precursor) to address vitamin A deficiency, which causes blindness and death in hundreds of thousands of children annually in developing countries.

**Proponents argue:**
- Could save hundreds of thousands of lives
- Addresses nutritional deficiency where dietary diversity is limited
- Distributed free to subsistence farmers
- Scientific consensus on safety

**Opponents argue:**
- Addresses symptom, not root cause (poverty, lack of diversity)
- Concerns about corporate control of food supply
- Long-term ecological effects unknown (precautionary principle)
- Reduces incentive to address systemic issues
- Perpetuates dependency rather than sovereignty

**As a government health minister, would you approve Golden Rice distribution in your country?**

#### Stakeholder Analysis

| Stakeholder | Interests | Power Level |
|-------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Malnourished Children** | Health, survival | None (voiceless) |
| **Parents** | Children's health, food security | Low |
| **Small Farmers** | Seed access, autonomy, livelihood | Low |
| **Biotech Companies** | Market acceptance, profits, reputation | High |
| **Scientists** | Research validation, addressing malnutrition | Medium |
| **Environmental Groups** | Ecological safety, precaution | Medium |
| **Food Sovereignty Advocates** | Local control, systemic change | Low-Medium |
| **Government** | Public health, economic development | High |

#### Ethical Framework Analysis

| Framework | Analysis | Likely Position |
|-----------|----------|-----------------|
| **Consequentialism** | Maximize wellbeing: saves lives, prevents suffering | **Approve** - greatest good for greatest number |
| **Deontology** | Duty to protect life, respect autonomy, precautionary principle | **Mixed** - conflicting duties |
| **Virtue Ethics** | Wisdom (both prudence and compassion needed), justice | **Mixed** - depends on which virtue prioritized |
| **Care Ethics** | Immediate care for suffering children vs. long-term care for communities | **Mixed** - different care relationships |
| **Environmental Ethics** | Precautionary principle, ecological integrity | **Oppose** - insufficient long-term data |
| **Food Sovereignty** | Self-determination, addressing root causes | **Oppose** - perpetuates dependency |

#### Discussion Questions

1. Is it ethical to use a technological fix for a problem caused by poverty and inequality?
2. How much certainty is needed before deploying GMOs widely?
3. Who should decide: experts, communities, governments?
4. Can we address the immediate crisis while working on systemic change?

### 6.3 Case Study 2: Efficiency vs. Animal Welfare

#### Scenario

You manage a mid-size egg farm. Currently, hens live in "enriched colony cages" - better than battery cages but still confined. You're considering three options:

**Option A: Status Quo (Colony Cages)**
- 8 hens per cage with some enrichments
- Efficient, predictable production
- Lower costs, competitive prices
- Annual profit: $120,000

**Option B: Cage-Free Barn**
- Hens free to move in large barn
- More labor, some efficiency loss
- Higher prices, smaller market
- Annual profit: $80,000
- Better animal welfare

**Option C: Pasture-Raised**
- Outdoor access, natural behaviors
- Significantly more land, labor, risk
- Premium prices, niche market
- Annual profit: $50,000
- Highest animal welfare
- Environmental exposure risks (predators, weather, disease)

**You have employees depending on farm income. What do you choose?**

#### Analyzing the Dilemma

```
COMPETING VALUES

Animal Welfare          Economic Viability
        │                       │
        │                       │
        ├───────────┬───────────┤
        │           │           │
        │      DECISION         │
        │           │           │
        ├───────────┴───────────┤
        │                       │
        │                       │
Environmental           Worker
Sustainability          Security
```

#### Stakeholder Perspectives

| Stakeholder | Preference | Reasoning |
|-------------|------------|-----------|
| **Hens** | Option C | Natural behaviors, welfare |
| **Animal Welfare Advocates** | Option C or B | Moral consideration for animals |
| **Budget Consumers** | Option A | Affordable protein |
| **Premium Consumers** | Option C | Willing to pay for welfare |
| **Farm Employees** | Option A | Job security, stable income |
| **Farm Owner (you)** | ??? | Balancing all considerations |
| **Environment** | Mixed | C has larger footprint but more natural |
| **Local Community** | Mixed | Economic stability vs. values |

#### Ethical Considerations

**Consequentialist:**
- Option A maximizes overall human wellbeing (affordable food, secure jobs)?
- Option C maximizes animal wellbeing?
- How to weigh human vs. animal interests?

**Deontological:**
- Do animals have rights to natural behaviors?
- Duty to employees and their families?
- Integrity: doing what you believe is right?

**Virtue Ethics:**
- What would a compassionate person do?
- What demonstrates good stewardship?
- Can you profit from suffering?

**Care Ethics:**
- You have care relationships with animals, employees, community
- How do you honor all care responsibilities?
- Is there a middle path?

#### Discussion Questions

1. Do animals have rights to express natural behaviors even if it costs humans money?
2. Is it ethical to keep animals in cages to provide affordable protein?
3. How much are you willing to sacrifice for animal welfare?
4. Could Option B be a transitional compromise?

### 6.4 Case Study 3: Water Rights and Agricultural Justice

#### Scenario

**Central Valley, California** - Severe drought year. River water must be allocated among:

**A. Large Industrial Almond Farms**
- 5,000 acres, export-oriented
- Extremely water-intensive crop
- Employ 200 workers
- Contribute $50 million to regional economy
- Owned by out-of-state investors

**B. Small Diversified Family Farms**
- 50 farms averaging 100 acres
- Vegetables, fruit, livestock
- Support 50 families directly
- Local food security
- Multigenerational local families
- Less water-efficient irrigation in some cases

**C. Endangered Salmon Ecosystem**
- Last wild salmon run in river
- Requires minimum flow for survival
- Cultural significance to Indigenous tribes
- Sport fishing economy
- Indicator of ecosystem health

**D. Urban Water District**
- 100,000 residents
- Drinking water, sanitation
- Low-income majority Latino community
- Already experience water shutoffs
- No affordable alternative source

**You're on the water board. How do you allocate limited water?**

#### Allocation Options

```
WATER ALLOCATION SCENARIOS

Total Available: 100,000 acre-feet

Option 1: Economic Priority
├─ Almond Farms: 60,000 (60%)
├─ Urban: 30,000 (30%)
├─ Small Farms: 8,000 (8%)
└─ Salmon: 2,000 (2%) - below survival threshold

Option 2: Human Priority
├─ Urban: 40,000 (40%)
├─ Small Farms: 35,000 (35%)
├─ Almond Farms: 20,000 (20%)
└─ Salmon: 5,000 (5%) - minimal survival

Option 3: Ecosystem Priority
├─ Salmon: 35,000 (35%) - healthy population
├─ Urban: 35,000 (35%)
├─ Small Farms: 20,000 (20%)
└─ Almond Farms: 10,000 (10%) - significant losses

Option 4: Equity Priority
├─ Each gets 25,000 (25%)
└─ Forces all to adapt/conserve
```

#### Ethical Dimensions

**Environmental:**
- Intrinsic value of salmon and ecosystem?
- Intergenerational duty to preserve species?
- Ecocentric vs. anthropocentric values?

**Economic:**
- Economic efficiency vs. equity?
- Local vs. regional vs. export economy?
- Who should bear costs of scarcity?

**Social:**
- Human right to water for basic needs?
- Historical and cultural connections?
- Indigenous treaty rights?
- Immigrant farmworker welfare?
- Wealthy investors vs. poor communities?

**Cultural:**
- Salmon as cultural keystone for tribes?
- Farming as way of life and identity?
- Urban community cohesion?

#### Discussion Questions

1. Should water go to highest economic use, or are there other priorities?
2. Do ecosystems have rights to water even if it costs humans?
3. How do we weigh indigenous treaty rights and cultural significance?
4. What responsibility do almond farmers have for crop choice in desert climate?
5. Is there a role for sacrifice and shared burden?

### 6.5 Case Study 4: Fair Labor or Farm Survival?

#### Scenario

You run a 40-acre organic vegetable farm. Currently, you employ 6 seasonal workers (March-October) at $15/hour with no benefits. Living wage in your area is $22/hour plus benefits.

**Workers are:**
- Mostly immigrants, some undocumented
- Experienced, skilled, reliable
- Work hard physical labor in all weather
- Many have families to support
- Limited other employment options

**Your situation:**
- Operate on thin margins (5% profit)
- Competing with conventional farms paying less
- Loyal customer base, but price-sensitive
- Could raise prices 30% to afford living wages
- Risk losing 50% of customers
- Alternative: Reduce workforce, mechanize, or close

**What do you do?**

#### Options Analysis

| Option | Impact on Workers | Impact on Farm | Ethical Trade-offs |
|--------|------------------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Status Quo** | Continue low wages | Survive | Exploit workers' vulnerability |
| **Living Wage** | Dignity, security | Risk closure | Possible loss of all jobs |
| **Partial Increase** | Modest improvement | Squeezed margins | Compromise principle, still inadequate |
| **Mechanize** | Fewer jobs | More viable | Efficiency over people |
| **Advocate Policy** | Systemic change | Uncertain timeline | Doesn't address immediate need |

#### Deeper Complications

**Question 1: If you can't afford to pay living wage, should you be in business?**
- Capitalist view: Market determines viability
- Justice view: No business model built on exploitation is acceptable
- Pragmatic view: Incremental change is realistic

**Question 2: Who is responsible?**
- Individual farm: Paying workers
- Consumers: Paying true cost
- Government: Safety net, regulations
- Society: Valuing farmers and farmworkers
- System: Food prices don't reflect true costs

**Question 3: Are you complicit or victim?**
- Perpetuating exploitation even if unintentionally?
- Or trapped in unjust system you didn't create?
- Can you be both?

#### Stakeholder Dialogue

```
WORKER: "I work 60 hours per week and still can't afford rent.
         My children deserve better."

FARMER: "I want to pay more, but I'll go bankrupt and then
        everyone loses their jobs."

CONSUMER: "I can't afford to pay more for food. I'm struggling too."

POLICY MAKER: "We can't mandate wages that put farms out of business."

ACTIVIST: "A business model based on poverty wages is unethical,
          period."

ECONOMIST: "The market will correct itself through supply and demand."

What do YOU say?
```

#### Discussion Questions

1. Is it ethical to run a business that can't pay living wages?
2. Who should sacrifice: workers, farmers, consumers, taxpayers?
3. How do we address systemic problems while meeting immediate needs?
4. What would you do if you were the farmer?

### 6.6 Synthesis: Common Themes

#### Recurring Ethical Challenges

| Challenge | Examples from Cases |
|-----------|---------------------|
| **Short-term vs. Long-term** | Golden Rice (immediate lives vs. systemic change), Water (this year vs. future) |
| **Individual vs. Systemic** | Farm labor (one farm vs. food system), all cases |
| **Competing Rights** | Water (human, animal, ecosystem rights), Animal welfare (hen vs. human interests) |
| **Power Imbalances** | All cases feature vulnerable populations with less voice |
| **Trade-offs** | All require sacrificing some goods for others |
| **Uncertainty** | Golden Rice (unknown effects), Water (climate future) |

Through case analysis, you've practiced:
- Identifying core ethical questions
- Analyzing from multiple perspectives
- Applying ethical frameworks
- Tolerating ambiguity
- Defending positions with reasons
- Recognizing complexity
- Developing practical judgment

## Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Stakeholder Analysis** | Identifying all parties affected by a decision and their interests |
| **Ethical Dilemma** | Situation where multiple values conflict with no clear right answer |
| **Trade-off** | Accepting less of one thing to get more of another |
| **Systemic Problem** | Issue rooted in structures and systems, not just individual actions |
| **Precautionary Principle** | Taking preventive action despite scientific uncertainty |
| **Moral Agency** | Capacity to make ethical decisions and be held accountable |
| **Practical Wisdom** | Ability to discern right action in complex situations (Aristotle's phronesis) |
