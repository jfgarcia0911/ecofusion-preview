## Lesson Content

### 5.1 Defining Food Justice

#### Core Principles

**Food Justice:** Ensuring that the benefits and burdens of the food system are distributed equitably, addressing systemic inequities, and empowering communities to participate in food system transformation.

**Key Elements:**

```
FOOD JUSTICE FRAMEWORK

┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│        FOOD JUSTICE                     │
└────────┬────────────────────────────────┘
         │
         ├──► EQUITY
         │    └─ Fair distribution of resources
         │       and opportunities
         │
         ├──► ACCESS
         │    └─ Healthy food available and
         │       affordable for all
         │
         ├──► POWER
         │    └─ Communities control their
         │       food systems
         │
         ├──► DIGNITY
         │    └─ Respect for all people in
         │       food system
         │
         ├──► SUSTAINABILITY
         │    └─ Environmental, economic,
         │       social viability
         │
         └──► TRANSFORMATION
              └─ Systemic change, not just
                 charity or service
```

#### Food Justice vs. Food Security

| Aspect | Food Security | Food Justice |
|--------|--------------|--------------|
| **Question** | Do people have enough food? | Why don't people have enough food? |
| **Focus** | Individual access | Systemic inequities |
| **Solution** | Provide food | Transform system |
| **Approach** | Service/charity | Rights/empowerment |
| **Change** | Symptoms | Root causes |
| **Power** | Maintains status quo | Redistributes power |

### 5.2 Historical Roots of Food Inequity

#### Colonialism and Land Dispossession

**Indigenous Peoples:**
- Forced removal from ancestral lands
- Destruction of traditional food systems
- Prohibition of hunting, fishing, gathering rights
- Forced assimilation and dietary change
- Ongoing impacts: health disparities, food insecurity

```
COLONIZATION'S FOOD SYSTEM IMPACTS

Before Colonization
├─ Indigenous peoples manage lands for millennia
├─ Diverse, sustainable food systems
├─ Food sovereignty and security
└─ Cultural, spiritual food connections

Colonization Process
├─ Land theft and forced removal
├─ Destruction of food sources (buffalo, salmon)
├─ Prohibition of traditional practices
├─ Forced agriculture and Christianity
└─ Boarding schools separate children from food culture

Ongoing Impacts
├─ Loss of land base (97% of Indigenous lands)
├─ Poverty and food insecurity
├─ Health crises (diabetes, obesity)
├─ Cultural disconnection
└─ Environmental degradation of remaining lands
```

#### Slavery and African American Agriculture

**Enslavement Era:**
- Enslaved Africans' agricultural knowledge exploited
- Denied fruits of their labor
- Forbidden to own land
- Brought food traditions that shaped American cuisine
- Gardens as resistance and cultural preservation

**Post-Emancipation:**
- 40 acres and a mule - promised but not delivered
- Brief period of Black land ownership
- Systematic land theft and violence
- Loss of Black-owned farmland: 14 million acres (1910) to 2 million acres (today)

**Mechanisms of Land Loss:**
- Violence and intimidation
- Discriminatory lending (USDA discrimination)
- Heirs' property issues
- Tax sales
- Eminent domain
- Contract fraud

#### Bracero Program and Farmworker Exploitation

**1942-1964:**
- Mexican laborers brought to fill WWII labor shortage
- Substandard wages and conditions
- No legal protections
- Created dependence on exploitable labor
- Pattern continues with H-2A program today

### 5.3 Environmental Racism in Food and Agriculture

#### Definition

**Environmental Racism:** Disproportionate impact of environmental hazards on people of color and low-income communities through discriminatory policies and practices.

#### Manifestations in Agriculture

| Issue | Description | Communities Affected |
|-------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Pesticide Exposure** | Farmworker exposure, drift to nearby homes | Latino, Black farmworkers and rural residents |
| **Factory Farms** | CAFOs sited near communities of color | Black, Latino, Native communities in rural areas |
| **Food Deserts** | Lack of healthy food in neighborhoods | Urban communities of color |
| **Pollution** | Agricultural runoff, air quality | Downwind/downstream communities |
| **Land Contamination** | Legacy pollution limits urban agriculture | Industrial, formerly redlined areas |
| **Climate Impacts** | Extreme weather affecting agriculture | Black, Indigenous farmers, Global South |

```
ENVIRONMENTAL RACISM CYCLE

Historical Discrimination
├─ Redlining
├─ Segregation
└─ Disinvestment
     │
     ▼
Vulnerable Communities
├─ Low property values
├─ Less political power
└─ Fewer resources
     │
     ▼
Hazard Siting
├─ Factory farms
├─ Waste facilities
└─ Industrial operations
     │
     ▼
Health Impacts
├─ Respiratory disease
├─ Cancer
└─ Chronic conditions
     │
     ▼
Reinforced Inequity
├─ Medical costs
├─ Lost work
└─ Property value decline
     │
     └──► Cycle continues
```

#### Case Study: Pesticide Drift in California

**Facts:**
- Farmworkers and nearby residents (majority Latino) experience pesticide exposure
- Schools fumigated with children present
- Higher rates of birth defects, cancers, respiratory disease
- Workers lack power to refuse dangerous work
- Weak enforcement of protective regulations

**Ethical Questions:**
- Is it acceptable to expose some people to harm to produce cheap food?
- Who benefits and who pays the costs?
- What are our obligations to protect vulnerable workers?
- How do we balance agricultural productivity with health justice?

#### Case Study: CAFO Siting in North Carolina

**Facts:**
- Hog CAFOs disproportionately located near Black, Latino, and Native American communities
- Lagoons of waste create air and water pollution
- Residents experience health problems, quality of life decline
- Communities had little say in siting decisions
- Economic benefits flow elsewhere

**Legal Actions:**
- Communities sued and won nuisance cases
- Documented environmental racism
- Industry lobbying for "right to farm" laws to shield from accountability

### 5.4 Intersectionality in Food Justice

#### Framework

**Intersectionality** (Kimberlé Crenshaw): Understanding how multiple forms of oppression (race, class, gender, etc.) intersect and compound.

```
INTERSECTIONAL ANALYSIS OF FOOD SYSTEM

Individual experiences oppression based on:

RACE + CLASS + GENDER + IMMIGRATION STATUS +
DISABILITY + GEOGRAPHY + LANGUAGE + AGE

         ┌─────────────────────┐
         │  Example: Latina    │
         │  Farmworker         │
         └────────┬────────────┘
                  │
    ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
    │             │             │
    ▼             ▼             ▼
  Racism      Sexism       Economic
  & Xenophobia in Wage Gap  Exploitation
  in Labor    & Sexual     & Poverty
  Markets     Harassment   Wages
    │             │             │
    └─────────────┴─────────────┘
              │
              ▼
        Compounded Vulnerability
        & Barriers to Justice
```

#### Intersectional Food System Issues

| Identity Intersection | Unique Challenges |
|----------------------|-------------------|
| **Women of Color in Farming** | Less access to land, credit; juggle farm and care work; underrepresented |
| **Disabled + Low-Income** | Limited mobility to access food; higher food costs for special diets |
| **Indigenous + Rural** | Land dispossession; food deserts on reservations; cultural foods unavailable |
| **Immigrant + Farmworker** | Exploited labor; fear of deportation; language barriers; no benefits |
| **LGBTQ+ + Rural** | Discrimination; lack of support; isolation; land access barriers |
| **Elderly + Urban** | Fixed income; mobility challenges; gentrification; isolation |

### 5.5 Food Justice Movements

#### Historical Movements

**Black Panther Party Free Breakfast Program (1969)**
- Fed thousands of children before school
- Demanded government action (led to federal breakfast program)
- Connected food to broader liberation struggle

**United Farm Workers (1962-present)**
- Cesar Chavez, Dolores Huerta, Filipino farmworkers
- Strikes, boycotts for worker rights
- Continues fighting for farmworker justice

**American Indian Movement (1968-present)**
- Cultural revitalization including food systems
- Land back and treaty rights
- Traditional foods as sovereignty

#### Contemporary Movements


#### Movement Strategies

| Strategy | Description | Examples |
|----------|-------------|----------|
| **Mutual Aid** | Community self-help and solidarity | Food pantries, community fridges, meal programs |
| **Direct Action** | Confronting power directly | Boycotts, strikes, protests |

### 5.6 Power, Privilege, and Positionality

#### Examining Privilege

**Food System Privilege Checklist:**

Do you:
- Always have enough food?
- Have transportation to grocery stores?
- Live near full-service supermarkets?
- Afford organic, specialty, or fresh foods?
- Have time to shop and cook?
- Have a kitchen with appliances?
- Read food labels easily (language, literacy)?
- Access culturally appropriate foods?
- Farm without fear of deportation?
- Own or access farmland?

**Reflection:**
- Which privileges do you hold?
- How do they shape your food experiences?
- How can you use privilege for justice?

#### Positionality and Allyship

**Positionality:** Understanding your social location (race, class, gender, etc.) and how it shapes your perspective and power.

**Authentic Allyship:**

```
ALLYSHIP PRINCIPLES

DO:
├─ Listen to and center affected communities
├─ Use privilege to amplify marginalized voices
├─ Take direction from community leaders
├─ Share resources and power
├─ Educate yourself
├─ Accept feedback and accountability
├─ Show up consistently
└─ Take risks and make mistakes, learn

DON'T:
├─ Center yourself or seek praise
├─ Speak for or over affected communities
├─ Assume you know what's best
├─ Extract stories or labor
├─ Performative "helping"
├─ Get defensive when challenged
├─ Show up only when convenient
└─ Expect communities to educate you
```

### 5.7 Transformative Justice in Food Systems

#### Principles

**Transformative Justice:** Addressing harm and creating accountability through community-led processes that transform conditions allowing harm.

**Applied to Food Systems:**
- Address root causes of inequity
- Center those most impacted
- Build community capacity and power
- Transform relationships and structures
- Accountability without punishment/exclusion
- Healing and restoration

#### Reparations and Land Justice

**Arguments for Reparations:**
- Stolen labor and land created wealth
- Ongoing impacts of historical injustice
- Moral obligation to repair harm
- Economic justice requires redistribution

**Forms:**
- Direct payments to descendants of enslaved people
- Land return to Indigenous peoples
- Debt forgiveness for Black farmers
- Preferential access to resources and programs
- Memorialization and truth-telling

**Land Back Movement:**
- Return of Indigenous lands to Indigenous control
- Co-management of public lands
- Free access for cultural practices
- Sovereignty and self-determination
