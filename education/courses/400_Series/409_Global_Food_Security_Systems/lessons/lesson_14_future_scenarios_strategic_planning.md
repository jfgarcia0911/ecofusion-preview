# Lesson 14: Future Scenarios and Strategic Planning

## Introduction

Food security and agriculture face unprecedented uncertainty—from climate change impacts to technological disruption, from geopolitical shifts to evolving consumer preferences. Strategic planning for the next 25 years requires not predicting a single future, but preparing for multiple possible scenarios. This final lesson examines scenario planning methodologies, explores plausible futures for food security and CEA through 2050, and provides frameworks for developing robust strategies that perform across diverse futures.

## Learning Objectives

1. Apply scenario planning methodologies to food security and CEA
2. Analyze key drivers of change affecting future food systems
3. Develop plausible scenarios for 2050 food security
4. Evaluate CEA's role across different future scenarios
5. Create adaptive strategies robust to multiple futures
6. Identify early warning indicators for scenario divergence
7. Design long-term strategic plans for food security transformation

## 1. Scenario Planning Framework

### What is Scenario Planning?

**Definition:** Structured method for thinking about and preparing for multiple plausible futures

**Purpose:**
- Acknowledge uncertainty (not prediction)
- Explore range of possibilities
- Test strategies across scenarios
- Identify robust strategies (work in multiple futures)
- Build adaptive capacity

**Not:**
- Forecasting (single predicted future)
- Preferred future (wishful thinking)
- All possible futures (infinite)

**Process:**
1. Identify focal question
2. Analyze key driving forces
3. Determine critical uncertainties
4. Develop scenario frameworks
5. Flesh out scenario narratives
6. Explore implications
7. Identify strategic options
8. Define indicators

### Key Driving Forces

**Demographic:**
- Population growth rates
- Urbanization trajectories
- Migration patterns
- Age structure changes
- Household composition

**Economic:**
- Global economic growth
- Income distribution
- Trade patterns
- Technology costs
- Resource prices

**Technological:**
- Agricultural innovation pace
- CEA technology advancement
- Automation and AI
- Biotechnology (genetics, synthetic biology)
- Digital agriculture

**Environmental:**
- Climate change trajectory
- Water scarcity
- Land degradation
- Biodiversity loss
- Resource depletion

**Political and Governance:**
- Globalization vs. nationalism
- Multilateral cooperation
- Governance effectiveness
- Regulatory environments
- Conflict and stability

**Social and Cultural:**
- Dietary preferences
- Consumer values (sustainability, health)
- Social movements
- Gender equity
- Knowledge and education

### Critical Uncertainties

**Identify factors that are:**
1. **Highly uncertain** (could go different ways)
2. **High impact** (significantly affect focal question)

**Examples for Food Security and CEA:**

**Uncertainty 1: Climate Change Severity**
- Optimistic: <2°C warming by 2100 (strong mitigation)
- Pessimistic: >3.5°C warming (limited action)
- Impact: Agricultural productivity, water availability, disasters

**Uncertainty 2: Technology Advancement**
- Rapid: CEA costs decline 60-80%, capabilities expand
- Slow: Incremental improvements 20-30%, limited expansion
- Impact: CEA competitiveness, adoption, contribution to food security

**Uncertainty 3: Globalization**
- Continued/expanded: Integrated global food markets, technology transfer
- Fragmented: Regional blocs, protectionism, limited cooperation
- Impact: Food trade, technology access, development assistance

**Uncertainty 4: Social Equity**
- Progressive: Reducing inequality, inclusive growth
- Regressive: Widening gaps, elite capture
- Impact: Who benefits from food systems, hunger trends

### Scenario Framework

**Common Approach: 2x2 Matrix**

**Select two critical uncertainties as axes:**
- X-axis: Climate Change Severity (Low to High)
- Y-axis: CEA Technology Advancement (Slow to Rapid)

**Results in 4 Scenarios:**

```
                         Rapid CEA Technology

High Climate Impact    |    Scenario 1     |    Scenario 2
                       |  "Tech Saves Us"  | "Green Revolution"
                       |                   |
                       |-------------------|-------------------
                       |                   |
Low Climate Impact     |    Scenario 3     |    Scenario 4
                       |  "Slow Transition"| "Business as Usual"

                       Slow CEA Technology
```

**Each scenario explores a plausible future given that combination**

## 2. Scenarios for 2050

### Scenario 1: "Tech Saves Us"

**Context:**
- Severe climate change (>3°C warming by 2100)
- Rapid CEA and agricultural technology advancement
- Mixed globalization (technology transfer but limited cooperation)
- Moderate inequality

**Food Security Situation:**
- Traditional agriculture severely disrupted (yields -20-40% in many regions)
- CEA expands dramatically (20-30% of vegetable supply globally)
- Urban food systems robust; rural areas struggle
- Significant internal migration to cities
- Some regions (small islands, deserts) dependent on CEA for survival

**CEA Characteristics:**
- Costs declined 70% (vertical farms $0.80-1.20/kg)
- Highly automated (minimal labor)
- Renewable energy integrated (80%+ of new facilities)
- Expanded crop range (includes some fruits, limited grains)
- Dominant in wealthy and climate-stressed regions

**Winners and Losers:**
- Winners: Urban populations, wealthy countries, technology companies
- Losers: Rural agricultural communities (displacement), poor countries (technology gap)
- Food security: Improved in urban areas and climate-stressed regions; worsened in isolated rural areas

**Critical Challenges:**
- Rural-urban divide and displacement
- Access for poorest populations (affordability despite cost reductions)
- Environmental justice (climate impacts unequal)
- Technology dependency risks

### Scenario 2: "Green Revolution"

**Context:**
- Moderate climate change (2-2.5°C by 2100)
- Rapid CEA and agricultural technology advancement
- Strong globalization and international cooperation
- Progressive equity (reducing inequality)

**Food Security Situation:**
- Traditional agriculture adapting with climate-smart practices
- CEA complements field agriculture (15-20% of vegetables)
- Technology transfer to developing countries successful
- Hunger declining to <300 million globally (from 735M in 2022)
- Nutrition improving broadly

**CEA Characteristics:**
- Costs declined 70%
- Diverse deployment models (community, commercial, smallholder)
- Appropriate technology emphasis (not just high-tech)
- Integrated with traditional agriculture
- Equitable access (subsidies, cooperatives, local ownership)

**Winners and Losers:**
- Winners: Broadly distributed (inclusive growth)
- Smallholder farmers integrated into CEA value chains
- Vulnerable populations prioritized
- Food security: Improved for most populations globally

**Critical Opportunities:**
- Synergy between traditional and CEA agriculture
- Inclusive innovation and technology transfer
- Climate adaptation successful
- Nutrition transition positive (healthy diets accessible)

**This is the aspirational scenario—requires sustained policy commitment and cooperation**

### Scenario 3: "Slow Transition"

**Context:**
- Moderate climate change (2-2.5°C by 2100)
- Slow CEA technology advancement (30% cost reduction)
- Fragmented globalization (regional focus)
- Moderate inequality

**Food Security Situation:**
- Traditional agriculture slowly adapting
- CEA remains niche (5-10% of vegetables)
- Food security progress slow (hunger ~600-700 million)
- Regional variations large (some regions advancing, others stagnant)
- Malnutrition persists, especially micronutrient deficiencies

**CEA Characteristics:**
- Costs high (vertical farms $2.00-3.00/kg)
- Limited primarily to wealthy countries and specific applications
- Greenhouse dominance (more affordable than vertical)
- Technology transfer limited
- Slow scaling

**Winners and Losers:**
- Winners: Wealthy urban populations (access to CEA premium products)
- Losers: Poor and rural populations (limited benefit)
- Food security: Mixed outcomes, inequitable

**Critical Challenges:**
- Inadequate response to climate and population pressures
- Technology remains expensive and elite
- Missed opportunities for transformation
- Persistent hunger and malnutrition

### Scenario 4: "Fragmented World"

**Context:**
- Severe climate change (>3.5°C by 2100)
- Slow CEA technology advancement
- Highly fragmented (nationalism, conflict, limited cooperation)
- High inequality

**Food Security Situation:**
- Traditional agriculture in crisis (yields -30-50% many regions)
- CEA insufficient scale/affordability to compensate
- Hunger increases to 1+ billion
- Acute crises and famines frequent
- Mass migration and conflicts over resources
- Food used as political weapon

**CEA Characteristics:**
- Costs remain high
- Deployed primarily for elite consumption and strategic self-sufficiency
- Military and government priority (food security as national security)
- Limited humanitarian use (insufficient scale)
- Technology hoarding (intellectual property barriers)

**Winners and Losers:**
- Winners: Very few (elites with CEA access, fortified regions)
- Losers: Majority of global population
- Food security: Catastrophic for billions

**This is the dystopian scenario—to be avoided through proactive action**

## 3. Implications Across Scenarios

### Role of CEA

| Aspect | Business as Usual | Slow Transition | Green Revolution | Tech Saves Us | Fragmented World |
|--------|------------------|-----------------|------------------|---------------|------------------|
| CEA % of vegetables | 3-5% | 5-10% | 15-20% | 20-30% | 8-12% (elite) |
| Accessibility | Elite/niche | Niche | Inclusive | Urban majority | Very limited |
| Technology | High-cost | Mid-cost | Affordable | Very affordable | High-cost |
| Integration | Separate | Complementary | Synergistic | Dominant urban | Strategic only |
| Global reach | Limited | Uneven | Widespread | Urban-focused | Fragmented |

**Key Insight:** CEA contribution to food security highly dependent on technology advancement AND equity/access strategies

### Investment Implications

**Scenario 1 (Tech Saves Us):**
- High commercial investment in CEA (tens of billions annually)
- Public investment in rural adaptation and migration support
- Climate resilience infrastructure

**Scenario 2 (Green Revolution):**
- Balanced public and private CEA investment
- Substantial international development assistance
- Technology transfer mechanisms
- Rural and urban integration

**Scenario 3 (Slow Transition):**
- Limited commercial CEA investment (niche markets)
- Conventional agriculture remains primary investment
- Slow innovation

**Scenario 4 (Fragmented World):**
- Strategic government CEA investment (food security as national security)
- Limited humanitarian funding (overwhelmed by needs)
- Private investment minimal (high risk)

### Policy Priorities

**All Scenarios Benefit From:**
- Climate change mitigation (reduces likelihood of worst scenarios)
- CEA technology R&D (increases options in all scenarios)
- Equity and inclusion policies (ensures broad benefits)
- International cooperation (enables best outcomes)

**Scenario-Specific:**
- Tech Saves Us: Rural transition support, affordability programs
- Green Revolution: Technology transfer, development assistance
- Slow Transition: Accelerate innovation, scale traditional adaptation
- Fragmented World: Prevention (cooperation, conflict resolution, equity)

## 4. Robust Strategies

### Strategy Testing

**Process:**
1. Develop strategy options
2. Test each against all scenarios
3. Evaluate performance (does it work? how well?)
4. Identify robust strategies (perform adequately across scenarios)

**Example Strategy: Large-Scale Public Investment in CEA**

**Performance by Scenario:**
- Business as Usual: Moderate benefit (infrastructure built, some food security improvement)
- Slow Transition: Low benefit (technology not viable, costly investment)
- Green Revolution: High benefit (accelerates positive trajectory)
- Tech Saves Us: High benefit (essential infrastructure early)
- Fragmented World: Moderate benefit (strategic food security, but limited reach)

**Conclusion:** Performs well in 3/5 scenarios, worth pursuing

### Adaptive Strategies

**Characteristics:**
- Flexible (can adjust as future unfolds)
- Diversified (multiple approaches, not all-in on one)
- Reversible (low-regret, can change course)
- Learning-oriented (monitor and adapt)

**Examples:**

**1. Portfolio Approach:**
- Invest in multiple CEA technologies (greenhouse, vertical, container)
- Support both commercial and community models
- Balance urban and rural interventions
- Hedge against uncertainty

**2. Phased Implementation:**
- Start with pilots and demonstrations
- Scale based on results and emerging trends
- Build infrastructure incrementally
- Allows course correction

**3. Build Adaptive Capacity:**
- Train diverse workforce (transferable skills)
- Develop flexible infrastructure (multi-purpose)
- Create learning systems (M&E, knowledge management)
- Foster innovation culture

**4. Scenario Planning Integration:**
- Monitor indicators signaling which scenario emerging
- Pre-planned responses for different scenarios
- Trigger points for strategy shifts
- Regular scenario review and update (every 2-3 years)

## 5. Early Warning Indicators

### Monitoring the Future

**Purpose:** Track indicators signaling which scenario is becoming more likely

**Climate Change Severity:**
- Global temperature anomaly trends
- Extreme weather event frequency
- Agricultural yield trends
- Water stress indicators

**Technology Advancement:**
- CEA cost trends ($/kg production cost)
- LED efficiency improvements
- Automation advancement
- Crop range expansion
- Investment flows into CEA technology

**Globalization:**
- Trade volumes and patterns
- Technology transfer rates
- International agreement adherence
- Conflict frequency

**Equity:**
- Income inequality trends (Gini coefficients)
- Hunger and malnutrition rates (disaggregated)
- Access to technology (digital divide, CEA access)

### Trigger Points

**Define in Advance:**
- If X indicator crosses Y threshold, then implement Z strategy

**Example:**
- If CEA production costs decline to <$1.50/kg: Accelerate scaling investment
- If hunger increases for 3 consecutive years: Intensify emergency CEA deployment
- If climate impacts exceed IPCC worst-case by 2030: Shift to Tech Saves Us strategies
- If international cooperation improves (measured by aid flows, agreements): Increase technology transfer programs

### Adaptive Management Cycle

**Annual Review:**
1. Update indicator data
2. Assess which scenario trending toward
3. Evaluate strategy performance
4. Adjust strategies as needed
5. Update scenarios if needed (major unexpected events)

**This makes scenarios a living tool, not one-time exercise**

## 6. Strategic Planning for 2050

### Vision Development

**Aspirational Goal:**
"By 2050, all people have reliable access to safe, nutritious, and affordable food produced sustainably and equitably, with CEA contributing significantly to resilient, climate-adapted food systems."

**Specific Targets (Green Revolution Scenario-aligned):**
- Hunger eliminated (zero people severely food insecure)
- Malnutrition in all forms reduced by 90%
- CEA provides 20% of global fresh vegetable needs
- 100% of urban areas have local CEA production within 10 km
- CEA accessible and affordable for all income levels (subsidized for poorest)
- Gender equity in agriculture and food systems achieved
- Net-zero carbon food systems
- 90% reduction in agricultural water use (globally)

### Pathways to Vision

**2025-2030: Foundation Phase**
- Accelerate CEA technology R&D (cost reduction, crop expansion)
- Policy frameworks enacted globally (200+ countries)
- Demonstration projects in 50+ countries
- Training 100,000+ CEA practitioners
- $50 billion investment mobilized
- Establish monitoring systems

**2030-2040: Scaling Phase**
- CEA reaches 10% of global vegetable supply
- Technology transfer to all developing regions
- Cost parity with field agriculture for key crops
- 1 million jobs in CEA globally
- Renewable energy standard (80%+ new facilities)
- Gender parity in CEA workforce and leadership

**2040-2050: Integration Phase**
- CEA 20% of vegetables, expanding to fruits
- Full integration with traditional agriculture
- Climate-resilient food systems globally
- Hunger and malnutrition near elimination
- Circular economy food systems (zero waste)
- Sustainable Development Goal 2 achieved

### Strategic Pillars

**1. Innovation and Technology:**
- R&D investment ($10B+ annually globally)
- Open-source technology platforms
- Appropriate technology emphasis
- Crop breeding for CEA

**2. Policy and Enabling Environment:**
- National food security strategies including CEA
- Supportive regulatory frameworks
- Incentive structures
- International cooperation mechanisms

**3. Investment and Finance:**
- Blended finance mechanisms
- Impact investment market development
- Public investment in public goods
- Risk mitigation instruments

**4. Capacity and Knowledge:**
- Training and education at scale
- Knowledge platforms and networks
- South-South exchange
- Research and learning systems

**5. Equity and Inclusion:**
- Gender-transformative approaches
- Marginalized population prioritization
- Affordable access strategies
- Community ownership models

**6. Integration and Systems:**
- CEA-traditional agriculture synergies
- Urban-rural linkages
- Circular economy (waste, energy, water)
- Climate adaptation mainstreaming

## Key Takeaways

1. **Multiple plausible futures exist** - scenario planning prepares for uncertainty rather than predicting single outcome

2. **Critical uncertainties shape outcomes** - climate severity, technology advancement, globalization, equity are key drivers

3. **CEA's role varies dramatically by scenario** - from niche (3-5%) to dominant urban food source (30%+)

4. **Robust strategies perform across scenarios** - portfolio approaches, adaptive management, learning systems

5. **Early warning indicators enable adaptation** - monitoring signals which future emerging, triggering strategy adjustments

6. **Vision and pathways guide action** - clear goals and phased approaches provide direction while remaining flexible

7. **Transformation requires multi-actor leadership** - governments, private sector, civil society, communities, individuals all essential
