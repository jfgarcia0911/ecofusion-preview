## 6.1 Fair Trade Foundations

### Core Principles

Fair Trade is built on ten principles established by the World Fair Trade Organization (WFTO):

```
10 PRINCIPLES OF FAIR TRADE
============================

1. CREATING OPPORTUNITIES
   Market access for marginalized producers

2. TRANSPARENCY & ACCOUNTABILITY
   Open management and commercial relations

3. FAIR TRADING PRACTICES
   Concern for social, economic, environmental well-being

4. FAIR PAYMENT
   Adequate wages, respect local minimum wage laws

5. NO CHILD LABOR, NO FORCED LABOR
   Adhere to UN Convention on Rights of the Child

6. NON-DISCRIMINATION, GENDER EQUITY, FREEDOM OF ASSOCIATION
   Equal opportunities, support women's empowerment

7. GOOD WORKING CONDITIONS
   Safe and healthy environment, comply with ILO conventions

8. CAPACITY BUILDING
   Develop producer independence and skills

9. PROMOTE FAIR TRADE
   Raise awareness and advocate for policy changes

10. RESPECT FOR THE ENVIRONMENT
    Environmental sustainability in production and sourcing
```

## 6.2 Fair Trade Certification Systems

### Major Certification Bodies

**1. Fair Trade USA**
- Largest certifier in North America
- Product certification model
- Minimum price + social premium
- Applicable products: coffee, cocoa, tea, sugar, produce, seafood

**Standards:**
```
FAIR TRADE USA STANDARDS
========================

MINIMUM PRICE FLOOR:
Product      Conventional    Organic      Social Premium
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Coffee       $1.40/lb        $1.70/lb     $0.20/lb
Cocoa        $2,400/MT       $2,600/MT    $240/MT
Sugar        $0.375/lb       $0.395/lb    $0.06/lb
Bananas      $10.30/box      $11.30/box   $1.00/box

ADDITIONAL REQUIREMENTS:
• Democratic organization (typically cooperatives)
• Environmental standards (pesticide reduction, water protection)
• Safe working conditions
• No child or forced labor
• Pre-financing available (up to 60% of contract)
```

**2. Fairtrade International (FLO)**
- Global certification system
- Operates in 70+ countries
- Network of national organizations
- Focus on smallholder farmer cooperatives

**Key Differences from Fair Trade USA:**
- More restrictive on plantation certification
- Stronger cooperative requirements
- Higher minimum prices for some products
- Different governance (multi-stakeholder vs. board)

**3. Fair Trade Federation**
- Membership organization
- Business certification (vs. product)
- 100% fair trade commitment required
- Self-reporting with peer review
- Focus on mission-driven enterprises

**4. World Fair Trade Organization (WFTO)**
- Organization certification
- Guarantee system for committed enterprises
- 10 Principles compliance
- Monitoring and verification
- Global reach, focus on artisan crafts and food

### Certification Process and Costs

**Steps to Certification:**

1. **Application** (1-2 months)
   - Eligibility review
   - Fee: $500-$2,000 initial application

2. **Audit** (2-4 months)
   - On-site visit by certifier
   - Document review
   - Interviews with staff and suppliers
   - Fee: $2,000-$15,000 depending on size

3. **Certification Decision** (1 month)
   - Approve, conditional approve, or deny
   - Corrective action plan if conditional

4. **Annual Renewal**
   - Annual fees based on sales volume
   - Periodic re-audits (every 3 years typical)
   - Fee: 1-3% of fair trade sales, minimum $1,000

**Total Cost Estimate:**
- Small business (< $1M fair trade sales): $3,000-$10,000/year
- Medium business ($1M-$10M): $10,000-$50,000/year
- Large business (> $10M): $50,000-$200,000+/year

### Criticism and Controversies

**Common Critiques:**
1. **Insufficient Price Premiums:** Minimum prices don't always cover production costs
2. **Certification Costs:** Barriers for smallest producers
3. **Limited Scale:** Only ~5-10% of global production certified
4. **Mission Drift:** Expansion to plantations, large corporations
5. **Complex Standards:** Difficult for farmers to understand and comply
6. **Market Concentration:** Still sold through conventional retail
7. **Limited Impact:** Studies show mixed results on poverty reduction

**Fair Trade Movement Response:**
- Continuous improvement of standards
- Investment in producer capacity building
- Transparency in pricing and premiums
- Research on impact and outcomes
- Advocacy for better trade policies
- Recognition of systemic issues beyond certification

## 6.3 Direct Trade Models

### What is Direct Trade?

Direct trade refers to companies sourcing directly from farmers/producers without intermediaries, emphasizing relationship, quality, and transparency.

**Characteristics:**
- Direct sourcing relationships (no middlemen)
- Quality-based premiums (not just minimum price)
- Long-term commitments
- Visits and relationship building
- Transparency in pricing
- Often exceeds fair trade minimums
- Not third-party certified (company defines standards)

### Direct Trade vs. Fair Trade

```
COMPARISON: DIRECT TRADE & FAIR TRADE
======================================

Aspect           Direct Trade           Fair Trade Certified
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Certification    None (self-defined)    Third-party verified
Standards        Company-specific       Standardized criteria
Price            Quality premium        Minimum floor + premium
Relationships    Direct, personal       Often through coops
Transparency     High (within company)  Public standards
Scale            Often smaller          Can be large scale
Cost             Lower overhead         Certification fees
Credibility      Company reputation     External verification
Flexibility      High                   Structured requirements
Governance       Company decides        Multi-stakeholder input
```

### Implementing Direct Trade

**Step-by-Step Process:**

**1. Origin Selection and Scouting**
- Research regions for quality and mission fit
- Visit potential farming communities
- Assess infrastructure and logistics
- Evaluate social and environmental context

**2. Farmer/Cooperative Identification**
- Quality evaluation (cupping/tasting for coffee, cacao)
- Farm visits and relationship building
- Assess practices and values alignment
- Understand farmer needs and challenges

**3. Price Negotiation and Agreement**
- Determine cost of production
- Establish quality premiums
- Set volume commitments
- Define long-term price mechanisms
- Contract terms (typically 3-5 years)

**4. Logistics and Import**
- Establish shipping/export relationships
- Quality control procedures
- Customs and compliance
- Payment terms and currency

**5. Relationship Maintenance**
- Regular visits and communication
- Technical support and training
- Feedback on quality
- Share business updates and forecasts
- Investment in community projects

**6. Transparency and Storytelling**
- Share pricing breakdown with customers
- Highlight farmer stories
- Document impact
- Differentiate brand through relationships

### Direct Trade Pricing Example

```
DIRECT TRADE COFFEE PRICING
============================

Farmer Costs (per lb green coffee):
────────────────────────────────────
Labor (harvest, processing)         $0.80
Inputs (organic fertilizer, etc.)   $0.25
Infrastructure (depreciatio)        $0.15
Cooperative overhead                $0.10
Certification costs (organic)       $0.05
────────────────────────────────────
Total Cost of Production            $1.35

Fair Income and Premiums:
────────────────────────────────────
Living wage profit margin           $0.45
Quality premium (exceptional)       $0.50
Sustainability premium (practices)  $0.30
Long-term relationship premium      $0.20
────────────────────────────────────
PRICE PAID TO FARMER COOP          $2.80/lb

Compare to:
- Commodity market price:           $1.20/lb
- Fair Trade minimum:               $1.60/lb
- Fair Trade organic:               $1.90/lb

Farmer receives 133% above fair trade organic,
enabling investment in quality, environment, community
```

### Case Study: Counter Culture Coffee

**Model:** Direct trade coffee roaster

**Approach:**
- Personal relationships with ~40 farmer partners globally
- Multi-year contracts with price above fair trade + quality bonuses
- Minimum 3 origin visits per year by staff
- Transparency reports detail pricing for each origin
- Pre-harvest financing to farmers
- Investment in community projects (schools, water, processing equipment)

**Impact:**
- Farmers earn 50-150% above fair trade minimums
- Long-term relationships (10+ years with many partners)
- Quality improvements benefit both parties
- Staff retention and engagement (mission-driven)
- Customer loyalty and premium pricing

**Challenges:**
- Resource intensive (travel, time, relationships)
- Difficult to scale while maintaining quality
- Dependent on key relationships (risk if farmer exits)
- No external verification of claims (trust-based)

## 6.4 Living Wage Commitments

### Defining Living Wage

A living wage is the minimum income necessary for a worker to meet their basic needs and those of their family, including food, housing, healthcare, education, and savings.

**Living Wage vs. Minimum Wage vs. Fair Wage:**

```
WAGE HIERARCHY
==============

MINIMUM WAGE
────────────────
Legal minimum set by government
Often insufficient for basic needs
Baseline compliance

FAIR WAGE
────────────────
Context-specific, considers:
• Local cost of living
• Industry standards
• Skill and experience
• Company profitability

LIVING WAGE
────────────────
Calculated based on actual costs:
• Food (nutritious diet)
• Housing (adequate shelter)
• Healthcare
• Education
• Transportation
• Clothing
• Small savings (emergencies)

THRIVING WAGE
────────────────
Beyond basics, includes:
• Quality of life
• Recreation and culture
• Long-term savings
• Economic security
```

### Calculating Living Wage

**Methodologies:**

**1. Global Living Wage Coalition**
Uses Anker Methodology:
- Cost of nutritious diet for family
- Decent housing cost
- Other essential expenses
- Provision for unexpected events (5-10%)
- Divided by full-time workers in household

**2. MIT Living Wage Calculator** (U.S. specific)
- County-level calculations
- Considers family composition
- Based on actual local costs
- Publicly available

**3. Fair Wage Network**
- Benchmarking approach
- Compares actual wages to living wage estimates
- Gap analysis and improvement plans

**Example Calculation:**

```
LIVING WAGE - RURAL COFFEE REGION (Example)
============================================

Monthly Household Expenses (4 persons: 2 adults, 2 children):

Food (nutritious diet)              $300
Housing (decent, secure)            $150
Healthcare (basic access)           $75
Education (children)                $50
Transportation                      $40
Utilities (water, electric)         $35
Clothing and household items        $30
Communication (phone)               $15
Emergency provision (10%)           $70
────────────────────────────────────────
TOTAL MONTHLY NEED                  $765

Assumption: 1.5 full-time workers in household

Living Wage per Worker = $765 / 1.5 = $510/month

If harvest season = 6 months of year:
Annual income needed: $510 × 12 = $6,120
Must earn during harvest: $6,120 / 6 = $1,020/month

Current average wage: $350/month during harvest
GAP: $670/month or 191% increase needed
```

### Implementing Living Wage Commitments

**1. Assess Current State**
- Calculate living wage for each geography
- Survey actual worker/farmer wages
- Identify gaps
- Prioritize by severity and scope

**2. Develop Timeline**
- Immediate improvements (low-hanging fruit)
- Medium-term roadmap (2-3 years)
- Full compliance target (often 5 years)
- Milestones and accountability

**3. Price and Business Model Implications**
- Calculate cost increase
- Evaluate pricing power
- Identify efficiency opportunities
- Consider hybrid funding (grants, impact investment)

**4. Supplier Engagement**
- Communicate commitment
- Collaborative gap-closing approach
- Technical and financial support
- Verification and monitoring

**5. Transparency and Reporting**
- Publish living wage commitment
- Annual progress reports
- Third-party verification
- Stakeholder feedback

## 6.5 Farmer Partnership Strategies

### Co-Investment Models

**Revenue Sharing:**
```
PROFIT SHARING STRUCTURE
========================

Example: Value-Added Processing Cooperative

Gross Revenue:                      $1,000,000

Operating Costs:
- Processing & packaging            $400,000
- Marketing & sales                 $150,000
- Administration                    $50,000
────────────────────────────────────────────
Net Profit:                         $400,000

Distribution:
- Farmer-owners (50%)               $200,000
- Working capital reserve (20%)     $80,000
- Community development fund (15%)  $60,000
- Worker bonuses (10%)              $40,000
- Admin reserves (5%)               $20,000
────────────────────────────────────────────
Total:                              $400,000

Impact: Farmers receive both initial payment
for product AND share of final profits
```

**Equity Ownership:**
- Farmer ownership stakes in processing/distribution company
- Governance rights proportional to ownership
- Long-term value creation and retention
- Examples: Equal Exchange, Organic Valley

**Co-Packing Arrangements:**
- Use farmer cooperative processing facilities
- Create jobs and value-add in origin communities
- Build local infrastructure and expertise

### Technical Assistance Programs

**Agronomic Support:**
- Extension services and training
- Demonstration farms
- Input support (seeds, equipment)
- Soil testing and recommendations
- Pest and disease management

**Business Development:**
- Financial literacy and management
- Cooperative governance training
- Market analysis and planning
- Quality control and grading
- Post-harvest handling

**Certification Support:**
- Organic transition assistance
- Fair trade or other certification fees
- Documentation and record-keeping
- Internal control systems for group certification

**Infrastructure Investment:**
- Processing equipment
- Storage facilities
- Water and irrigation systems
- Renewable energy installations
- Transportation and logistics

### Risk Sharing Mechanisms

**Price Protection:**
- Minimum price floor (protects farmers from low markets)
- Maximum price ceiling (protects business from high markets)
- Share risk of volatility

**Crop Insurance:**
- Company subsidizes or provides insurance
- Protects against weather, pests, disease
- Enables farmer investment and stability

**Volume Flexibility:**
- Committed minimums with take-or-pay
- Ability to sell surplus to company if available
- Market access guarantee

## Key Takeaways

1. Fair trade is built on principles of fair payment, transparency, good working conditions, and environmental sustainability
2. Major certification systems (Fair Trade USA, Fairtrade International, WFTO) provide third-party verification but differ in standards and approach
3. Direct trade models emphasize personal relationships, quality premiums, and transparency without third-party certification
4. Living wage commitments ensure workers and farmers can meet basic needs and live with dignity
5. Farmer partnership strategies include co-investment, equity ownership, technical assistance, and risk sharing
6. Ethical sourcing creates value for producers, businesses, consumers, and communities while addressing power imbalances in global trade
7. Implementation requires assessment, timeline development, supplier engagement, and transparent reporting

3. What are the limitations of voluntary certification systems in transforming global trade? What role should regulation play?

4. Can large-scale commodity chains ever be truly fair, or does ethical sourcing require small-scale, relationship-based models?

5. How can businesses verify farmer/worker wages and conditions without invasive auditing that undermines trust?

## Practical Exercise

**Fair Trade or Direct Trade Business Model Design**

**Part 1: Product Selection**
Choose a globally traded food product to source ethically (coffee, cocoa, tea, spices, sugar, etc.)

**Part 2: Certification vs. Direct Trade Decision**
- Evaluate pros/cons of fair trade certification for your product
- Evaluate pros/cons of direct trade approach
- Make recommendation with justification

**Part 3: Pricing and Value Share**
- Research conventional commodity price
- Calculate fair trade or living wage price
- Map value distribution across supply chain (farmer, exporter, importer, processor, retailer)
- Design improved value distribution
- Calculate cost implications for your business

**Part 4: Partnership Model**
- Define ideal farmer partner characteristics
- Outline partnership terms (price, contract length, volume, quality)
- Describe technical assistance or capacity building you'd provide
- Detail risk-sharing mechanisms
- Create farmer value proposition

**Part 5: Transparency and Communication**
- Design customer-facing transparency (label, website, etc.)
- Draft supplier story for marketing
- Create annual impact report outline
- Identify verification/credibility mechanisms
