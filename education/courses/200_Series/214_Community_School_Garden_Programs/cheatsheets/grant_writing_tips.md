## Course 214: Community & School Garden Programs

## Before You Write

### Research & Preparation

**Find the Right Funders:**
- ✓ Mission alignment with your project
- ✓ Geographic eligibility (local, state, national)
- ✓ Grant size matches your need
- ✓ Your organization is eligible applicant type
- ✓ Deadline is feasible for preparation
- ✓ Requirements are manageable

**Read Everything:**
- Grant guidelines (multiple times!)
- Funder's website and annual report
- Previously funded projects
- Application instructions
- Evaluation criteria
- Reporting requirements

**Gather Information:**
- Needs assessment data
- Organizational background documents
- Budget information
- Letters of support (if required)
- Tax documentation (501c3, EIN)
- Success stories and testimonials

## Writing Strong Proposals

### Executive Summary (First Impression Matters!)

**Include:**
- Organization name and mission
- Project name and purpose
- Target population and numbers
- Funding request amount
- Key anticipated outcomes

**Tips:**
- Write this LAST even though it appears first
- Keep to specified length (often 1 page)
- Use clear, compelling language
- Highlight alignment with funder priorities
- Make it stand-alone (reviewers may read only this)

**Example:**
> "[Organization] requests $15,000 from [Funder] to establish an educational garden serving 120 third-grade students at [School], a Title I elementary where 65% of students receive free/reduced lunch. The garden will provide hands-on science education addressing state standards while improving nutrition knowledge and vegetable consumption. By June 2027, 90% of participants will demonstrate improved understanding of plant life cycles and 70% will report trying new vegetables."

---

### Statement of Need (Make the Case)

**Structure:**
1. Present the problem or opportunity
2. Provide supporting data and evidence
3. Explain local context and impact
4. Connect to funder's priorities
5. Show why NOW and why YOUR organization

**Data to Include:**
- Relevant statistics (academic, health, community)
- Quotes from stakeholders
- Results of needs assessment
- Research on garden programs
- Demographic information
- Current gaps in services

**Tips:**
- Use credible sources (cite them!)
- Balance quantitative data with stories
- Focus on those you'll serve, not your organization
- Avoid jargon and acronyms
- Don't exaggerate or manipulate data
- Connect local need to broader issues

**Avoid:**
- ✗ "Everyone knows that..."
- ✗ Unsupported claims
- ✗ Relying only on anecdotes
- ✗ Making it all about your organization
- ✗ Negativity without solutions

### Project Description (The Heart of the Proposal)

**Use Clear Structure:**

**Goals & Objectives**
- 3-5 SMART goals
- Specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound
- Connected to stated need
- Aligned with funder priorities

**Activities & Methods**
- What will you DO?
- Step-by-step description
- Timeline for activities
- Who will be involved?
- Evidence-based approaches
- Reasonable scope

**Staffing & Roles**
- Who will lead the work?
- Qualifications and experience
- Time commitments
- Roles and responsibilities

**Timeline**
- Month-by-month or quarterly plan
- Key milestones
- Critical path identified
- Realistic given resources

**Tips:**
- Paint a picture - help reader SEE the program
- Use active voice ("We will build" not "Beds will be built")
- Be specific and detailed but concise
- Show you've thought through implementation
- Explain WHY you've chosen this approach
- Connect to research and best practices
- Demonstrate cultural responsiveness

**Example Activity Description:**
> "Each third-grade classroom (4 classes, 30 students each) will participate in weekly 45-minute garden-based science lessons aligned to state standards. The garden coordinator will co-teach with classroom teachers, providing 20 lessons throughout the school year covering plant life cycles, ecosystems, and scientific investigation. Students will maintain dedicated raised beds, conduct experiments, collect data, and present findings. Lessons follow the experiential learning cycle: hands-on experience, reflection, concept development, and application."

---

### Evaluation Plan (Prove It Works)

**Include:**
- How will you measure success?
- What data will you collect?
- When and how will data be collected?
- Who will collect and analyze data?
- How will you use findings to improve?
- How will you report results?

**Types of Data:**
- Participation numbers (attendance logs)
- Learning outcomes (pre/post tests, observations)
- Behavior change (surveys, self-reports)
- Satisfaction (surveys, feedback)
- Program quality (fidelity checks, observations)

**Tips:**
- Align metrics with stated goals
- Use both quantitative and qualitative
- Be realistic about what you can measure
- Include baseline data if available
- Show you'll use data for improvement, not just reporting
- Demonstrate valid and reliable methods

**Sample Metrics Table:**

| Goal | Metric | Method | Timeline |
|------|--------|--------|----------|
| Students demonstrate plant knowledge | % scoring 80%+ on assessment | Pre/post test | Sept & May |
| Students try new vegetables | % reporting trying 3+ new vegetables | Survey | Quarterly |
| Teachers integrate science | # of lessons taught | Logs | Monthly |
| Community engagement | # of volunteer hours | Sign-in sheets | Ongoing |

### Budget & Budget Narrative (Show the Money)

**Budget Categories:**

**Personnel**
- Salaries (with benefits if applicable)
- Consultants/contractors
- Training stipends

**Materials & Supplies**
- Tools and equipment
- Seeds and plants
- Soil and amendments
- Ongoing supplies

**Infrastructure**
- Raised beds
- Fencing
- Water system
- Storage shed
- Pathways

**Other Costs**
- Printing/copying
- Curriculum materials
- Event costs
- Evaluation expenses

**Budget Narrative Tips:**
- Justify EVERY line item
- Explain calculations (hourly rate × hours)
- Show cost-effectiveness
- Indicate other funding sources
- Include in-kind contributions
- Be realistic, not inflated or lowballed

**Example Budget Narrative:**
> "**Garden Coordinator Salary ($7,500):** Part-time coordinator will work 10 hours/week for 30 weeks at $25/hour. This position is essential for consistent programming, providing direct instruction to students, supporting teachers, coordinating volunteers, and managing maintenance. The rate is competitive for our region and reflects experience requirements. School budget will fund remaining 10 weeks of school year."

## Common Grant Writing Mistakes

### Content Mistakes

**❌ Don't:**
- Write vague, unmeasurable goals
- Copy-paste from another proposal without tailoring
- Use excessive jargon or acronyms
- Make unsupported claims
- Ignore the funder's priorities
- Propose unrealistic scope
- Forget to proofread
- Miss the deadline!

**✓ Do:**
- Customize every proposal
- Use clear, plain language
- Support claims with data
- Align tightly with funder
- Propose achievable scope
- Have multiple people review
- Submit early if possible

### Format Mistakes

**❌ Don't:**
- Ignore page limits or formatting requirements
- Use tiny fonts to fit more
- Forget page numbers
- Submit incomplete applications
- Overlook required attachments

**✓ Do:**
- Follow ALL instructions exactly
- Use readable fonts (11-12 pt)
- Include all required elements
- Check and double-check completeness
- Organize clearly with headings

## The Winning Formula

### CARL Framework

**C - Clear & Compelling**
- Is the need evident?
- Are goals specific and measurable?
- Can reviewers easily understand?

**A - Aligned**
- Does this match funder priorities?
- Is there clear connection to mission?
- Do activities lead to outcomes?

**R - Realistic**
- Is the scope achievable?
- Is the timeline reasonable?
- Is the budget justified?
- Does the team have capacity?

**L - Logical**
- Does the flow make sense?
- Do activities lead to outcomes?
- Is the evaluation appropriate?
- Does one section contradict another?

## After Submitting

### If You're Awarded

**Immediately:**
- Send thank you letter to program officer
- Review grant agreement carefully
- Set up tracking systems for budget and reporting
- Add reporting deadlines to calendar
- Communicate award to team and stakeholders

**Ongoing:**
- Keep detailed records
- Track expenses carefully
- Document activities and outcomes
- Communicate with funder (updates, challenges, successes)
- Submit reports on time
- Maintain relationship beyond grant period

### If You're Declined

**Do:**
- Request feedback (if offered)
- Thank them for consideration
- Learn from the experience
- Revise and reapply (if allowed)
- Try other funders
- Consider alternative approaches

**Don't:**
- Argue with decision
- Burn bridges
- Give up after one rejection
- Take it personally

## Top 10 Grant Writing Tips

1. **Start Early** - Don't wait until the last minute
2. **Follow Instructions** - Every. Single. One.
3. **Tell a Story** - Data + narrative = compelling
4. **Be Specific** - Concrete details beat vague claims
5. **Show Impact** - Focus on outcomes, not just activities
6. **Demonstrate Alignment** - Mirror funder's language and priorities
7. **Prove Feasibility** - Show you can actually do this
8. **Budget Realistically** - Neither inflated nor too low
9. **Write Clearly** - Avoid jargon, use active voice
10. **Get Feedback** - Fresh eyes catch what you miss

## Sample Grant Timeline

**8 Weeks Before Deadline:**
- Research funder thoroughly
- Review guidelines
- Confirm eligibility and fit
- Gather needed information

**6 Weeks Before:**
- Draft project description
- Develop budget
- Identify evaluation plan
- Request letters of support (if needed)

**4 Weeks Before:**
- Complete first full draft
- Get feedback from colleagues
- Revise based on feedback

**2 Weeks Before:**
- Finalize all sections
- Complete budget narrative
- Gather all attachments
- Final proofread

**1 Week Before:**
- Final review against guidelines
- Format and polish
- Assemble complete package
- Plan for submission

**3 Days Before:**
- Submit! (Don't wait until deadline)
- Save confirmation
- Breathe!
